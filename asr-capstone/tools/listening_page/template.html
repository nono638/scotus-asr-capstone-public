Listening bench

What this page is for

Seven speech recognition models transcribed the 61 oral arguments of the Supreme Court's October 2017 term. Wherever most of them disagree with the official transcript, or split among themselves, the place is flagged: 9,504 spots in all. A computer then sorted every spot by the kind of difference it is. Simple rules came first, and a language model (Gemma 4 31B, run locally) sorted the 819 spots the rules could not place.

Those counts can't be quoted until someone checks the sorting. That is this page: 200 of the flagged spots, picked at random. For each one, play the sentence clip, tick every one of eight short questions that is true of the highlighted words from what you hear, and say who got it right. The computer's labels are hidden here on purpose, so they can't sway you.

The rules for labelling, and why they are what they are, are in labelling-guidelines.md (version 0.2, a pilot).

Labels save as you click, in this browser and in your claude.ai account.

Labels are not reaching your claude.ai account from this view, so they are kept in this browser only. Use "Copy all labels" before you clear the browser or switch computers. Opening the page in claude.ai, signed in as its owner, should fix it.

One square per spot, in order. Green is labelled, blue is half done. The order is already random, so working straight through is fine; the squares are only for going back to one.

Keyboard shortcuts: 1–8 tick a question · 9 none of these · 0 can't tell · A S D F G who got it right · Q W E play the three clips · Space play again · ← → previous or next spot · N next unlabelled. Off while you type a note.

As a clip plays, each model's line highlights the word that model thinks is being spoken, from its own word timings; click a word to play from there. Where people talk over each other those timings can be off by a second or more, and words a model left out have nothing to highlight.

Sentences come from the official transcript. Clip times come from Whisper's word timings, so a clip can start or stop a little early or late. Qwen3 is shown first because it has the lowest error rate on the usual benchmark scoring.

Afterwards these labels are compared with two automatic sorts, neither shown here: simple rules written after a hand read of 157 spots, and a language model given the same eight questions and the official and model sentences as text. That model is Gemma 4 31B, run locally through Ollama. It is a mid-sized open model, not a frontier one such as Claude, and it never hears the audio.
