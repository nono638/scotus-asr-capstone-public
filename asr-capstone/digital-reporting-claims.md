# Digital reporting: what vendors claim, and how it went for courts and lawyers

Researched 17 October 2026, building on `vendor-claims.md` (which has the headline
claims: FTR's "up to 95%", Verbit's 99% after human review). Nothing from that file is
repeated here except where a new source changes it.

"Seen" means the page or PDF was opened and the words read from it. "Snippet" means I
only saw it in search results, so treat it as unconfirmed. Every source is labelled with
who it comes from and whether they have a stake.

## Summary

**Hunch 1: the claims get bigger off the beaten path. Partly true, but not where I
expected.** The biggest FTR number is not hidden. It is on FTR's own homepage: "98%
Speech-to-text accuracy with FTR RealTime", with no "up to", and the same page also
says 95%. Archived copies date the 98% to the site redesign in mid-2026. FTR's pages also
say things like "There's no need to recruit a stenographer to create a written record."
Other vendors have gone higher: Verbit's AI was described in 2020 as having "a claimed
over 99.9% accuracy", and Rev sells "99.9%+" transcripts with "No editing needed" (with
human checking). The claim "as accurate as a stenographer" comes mainly from AAERT, the
digital reporters' association, which FTR sponsors. The procurement record points the
other way. The one FTR contract I found (Ashtabula County, Ohio, 2025) promises no
accuracy at all, and New Mexico, an FTR customer that FTR holds up as a showcase, says
in a July 2026 policy that FTR real-time transcripts "May contain errors, omissions, or
inaccuracies" and are "not the official record". I found no documented private sales
pitch. The only reports of pitches come from the court reporters' side and give no
specifics, so they are hearsay.

**Hunch 2: many lawyers regretted digital reporting and went back to stenographers.
Not supported as stated.** I found no lawyer saying they used digital reporters, regretted
it and went back. Recording failures are real and documented: appeals courts have ordered
new trials because the recording was inaudible (Ohio 2024, North Carolina 2018), and there
are about ten first-hand complaints from lawyers. But nearly all of those complaints
reached me through court-reporter outlets, and most are about court recordings, not
depositions. Every claim that a whole state "went back" to stenographers (New Mexico,
Texas, Nevada, Illinois) traces to court-reporter advocacy lists, and I could not confirm
any of them. The overall direction is toward more recording: in 2026 eight states mandate
it and nineteen allow it statewide. The measured evidence I found is old and mixed: a
1983 federal study found transcripts from audio *more* accurate than stenographic ones,
though its design favoured audio. And the newest study, Texas in October 2026, found
that the courts using digital recording were satisfied, while judges and court reporters
in its survey were sceptical.

**How strong is this?** The vendor quotes are solid: they are in their own words, dated
and saved. The question of how well digital reporting works is badly under-measured.
The Texas study says plainly that "courts currently do not track quantitative data on
transcript processing times, error rates, or appeals." Both sides argue mostly from
anecdotes and from the same 1983 study. One big gap: Reddit refused every request, so the
everyday-lawyer forums were not checked at all.

Cost and time claims on the current homepage (seen, none with a source or method):
"Up to 90 % reduction in transcript wait times", "73 % reduction in IT costs for courts
after migrating to cloud storage", "97 % reduction in time spent reviewing proceedings",
"98 % reduction in search time". The court administrators page says "Save 60 - 80% when
you move from on-prem to cloud" (agent-seen). I found no dollar saving per courtroom from
FTR.

**What changed over time.** Two things. First, the 2022 figure came with a condition: 95%
only "with an FTR Smart Audio setup". Later material drops the condition. Second, the
2026 redesign added an unqualified 98% next to the old 95%. From 2010 to 2023 FTR's
homepage made no transcript accuracy claim at all, only counts of venues and countries.
The May 2026 homepage said "Capture a 100% accurate digital recording", which is about
the audio, not the text.

**FTR misquoting a survey.** FTR's site menu (seen) says: "Courts report that 76% of
scheduling delays and 55% of cost increases are directly tied to the shortage". The
source is AAERT's 2025 survey (seen), which says something different: "76% reporting that
it creates scheduling difficulties and 55% citing increased costs". Those were answers to
"What is the impact of not having enough stenographers?" The respondents were mostly
attorneys and legal staff, not courts, and the survey does not say 76% *of delays*.

**What buyers say.**
- **Ashtabula County, Ohio**, Resolution 2025-368 (28 Aug 2025, seen by the research
  agent), includes FTR's own "Proposal & Statement of Work" for RealTime: $8,808 per
  courtroom per year, $26,424 for three, "Maximum usage of 6 hours per business day". It
  has **no accuracy figure, guarantee or service level**. The procurement documents I
  could find are more careful than the website, not less.
- **New Mexico Judicial Branch**, policy approved by Order S-1-AO-2026-00025 (July 2026,
  seen): "Real-time transcripts generated from FTR recordings are not the official record
  of court proceedings." They are "automatically sealed upon creation", "May contain
  errors, omissions, or inaccuracies", and "Shall not be cited, quoted, or used in
  filings, appeals, or other judicial or administrative proceedings unless expressly
  authorized by court order." To be fair, it is not a rejection: judges may still use
  them internally, and the sealing is meant to last until a secure system lets counsel
  see them. But it is the court's own judgement that the draft is not a record, set
  beside FTR's 98%.
- **Alaska court RFP questions and answers** (snippet only): a bidder asked whether an
  "80–85% accuracy" AI option at a discount was acceptable, and the court answered "No".
  Not confirmed.

### Advocacy groups on the digital side (stake: for digital)

- **AAERT** (American Association of Electronic Reporters and Transcribers), 2025 Court
  Reporting Industry Trends Report (seen): "With proper training, oversight, and quality
  control measures, digital court reporters have proven to be just as accurate as their
  stenographer counterparts." The brief summary says digital reporting "matches
  stenography in accuracy". The report's only evidence is a survey of perceptions (550+
  respondents and 27 interviews, Q4 2024, recruitment not described); no accuracy test was
  run. FTR says it is an annual AAERT sponsor, and AAERT's 2016 white paper lists FTR,
  JAVS and VIQ among its contributors.
- **STTI** (Speech-to-Text Institute): its executive director Jim Cudahy said in 2019
  that "other technologies have proven capable of meeting that standard", the accuracy
  standard for court reporters (Virginia Lawyers Weekly, snippet only). Cudahy is a former
  NCRA executive director; Verbit invested in STTI (above).

### Claims made in private

I found none documented. The National Court Reporters Association (NCRA) wrote to bar
associations about "a tactic by some vendors", where a digital recorder is sent instead
of a stenographer and "This substitution is often not disclosed until the deposition is
ready to begin, at which time a stipulation is demanded" (2021-22 letter, seen by the
research agent). An NCRA member warned lawyers not to be "fooled by doublespeak, buzz
words, sales pitches" (Journal of Court Reporting, 25 Apr 2022, agent-seen). Neither
names a vendor, a date or the words of a pitch. These count as hearsay from an interested
party.

## 2. How it went for lawyers, judges and courts

### Studies and surveys

**Measured results**

- **Federal Judicial Center (FJC), *A Comparative Evaluation of Stenographic and Audiotape
  Methods for United States District Court Reporting*, July 1983** (seen). 12 courtrooms,
  4,213 hours. "Transcripts produced from records taken by the audio recording system were
  more accurate than the transcripts produced by the stenographic reporting method." Where
  the two transcripts disagreed, "the audio-based transcript matched the audiotape on 58
  percent of the discrepancies". On disagreements that judge and lawyer panels thought
  likely to matter, the split was 62% to 38%. But "the steno-based transcripts had fewer
  words misspelled". Cost: "$18,604 for an audio recording system, compared to $40,514"
  for stenography. **Caveat:** the tape was the answer key, and the audio-based transcript
  was typed from that same tape, which favours audio. The FJC itself warned the results
  would not hold where "responsibilities and procedures were not clearly defined".
  Stake: the federal judiciary's research arm, with a cost interest. **Both sides cite
  this study.** AAERT cites it for accuracy; NCRA cites it to argue recording needs
  trained staff to work.
- **Jones, Kalbfeld, Hancock and Clark, "Testifying While Black", *Language*, 2019**
  (snippet only). 27 Philadelphia court reporters averaged 82.9% accuracy transcribing
  African American English. This measures stenographers' errors, not recording, and is a
  reminder that the human baseline is not perfect either. Stake: academic.

**Official evaluations**

- **FJC, *Digital Audio Recording Technology: A Report on a Pilot Project in Twelve
  Federal Courts*, 14 May 1999** (seen). Experiences "ranged from a court that had only
  praise for digital recording to a court that had terminated its use of the system in
  deep frustration with the product". That court quit "after the vendor failed to provide
  software and equipment changes it had promised". Yet "all the pilot courts, even the
  court that terminated its participation in the pilot, recommended that digital recording
  be approved", and "in nearly every instance in which a transcript was requested, the
  transcript quality was satisfactory or very good." Mixed, but on balance favourable.
- **Texas Office of Court Administration / Texas A&M Public Policy Research Institute,
  digital court recording study, summary dated 1 Oct 2026** (seen). Ordered by the Texas
  legislature (House Bill 16). The most recent and most even-handed source I found.
  - Survey of 1,714 Texans plus 63 interviews: "Court reporters are the most resistant to
    DCR adoption, with roughly 89% disagreeing that DCR produces a record as accurate as a
    stenographic reporter. Judges express similar, but less stated, skepticism. Court
    recorders and appellate-level respondents are considerably more receptive." Only 8.9%
    of court reporters agreed; court recorders agreed "at a rate of about 69%". (Each
    group rates its own job highest.)
  - All groups agreed "that AI alone is insufficient to produce a legally sufficient
    record" and that a trained human must monitor any recording.
  - Courts observed using it: "all felt that the DCR system was sufficient for their needs
    and none of the courts reported an impact on appeals due to the record." One court
    reported "$80,000 in annual savings after the first year and reporting zero system
    failures over six years of operation" (a buyer's own report, relayed by the study).
  - Cost: digital recording about "$72,733 per courtroom annually" against $118,463 to
    $167,941 for a stenographer.
  - The key limitation: "courts currently do not track quantitative data on transcript
    processing times, error rates, or appeals." A direct comparison of filed transcripts
    "is impractical because errors are usually corrected before filing, and courts rarely
    retain audio for later comparison."
  - Recommendation: digital recording "should be viewed as a complement to the CSR
    workforce, not a replacement."
- **National Center for State Courts, *Making the Record*, Sept 2013** (seen, a copy
  hosted by AAERT). Six states (Alaska, Indiana, New Hampshire, Oregon, Utah, Vermont)
  use audio for all or most general-jurisdiction sessions; New Jersey about 90%, Kentucky
  video about 60%. It explicitly does not judge which method gives a better record.
- **Judicial Council of California, fact sheet, Jan 2025** (seen). An estimated 71.6% of
  family, probate and unlimited civil hearings had no verbatim record at all, because no
  reporter was available and recording was not allowed. Stake: the court system, which
  wants recording allowed.

**Surveys of users (stake noted)**

- **AAERT 2025 Trends Report** (seen; stake: pro-digital). Stenographers are still "the
  most utilized method ... with an 82% usage rate". Among people who chose a digital
  reporter, the top reason was faster delivery (30%), not accuracy (15%). It asks about
  perceptions only.
- **Ducker Worldwide, 2013-14, commissioned by NCRA** (seen by the research agent; stake:
  pro-steno). Says "stenographic court reporters remain the overwhelming choice of
  attorneys", but only about 12 of its 120 interviews were with law firms.
- **No survey found that asks lawyers directly whether they prefer stenographers or
  digital reporters.**

### Court decisions

- **State v. Riddlebarger, 2024-Ohio-5356** (Ohio 9th District, 12 Nov 2024, seen):
  "The beginning of the trial up to the first witness is almost completely inaudible.
  Although questions to the complaining witness are mostly audible, her answers are not."
  The court ordered "a new trial". It cites a similar case, Cleveland v. McGervey,
  2022-Ohio-3911, and two older cases where the *stenographic* record was lost (a
  reporter's illness; destroyed notes).
- **State v. Yates, 262 N.C. App. 139 (2018)**: a new trial after "a recording equipment
  malfunction prevented the court reporter from producing a full transcript", including
  cross-examination of the victim (UNC School of Government summary, seen). A reporter
  was present, so this is not a clean recording-versus-steno case.
- **State v. Hull (Ohio Court of Appeals, 30 Mar 2005)**: the transcript said "inaudible"
  "over 500 times" in 163 pages. A separate opinion advised lawyers, "when in doubt,
  obtain your own reporter" (quoted in NCRA's case list, seen).
- **Bradley v. Hazard Technology Co.** (Maryland Court of Appeals, 1995, seen): a faulty
  tape left gaps, but "the unavailability of a full transcript does not automatically
  entitle a party to a new trial", noting that "Electronic recording or stenographic
  equipment will occasionally fail".
- Snippet only: Manuel v. State (Fla. 1999), Collins v. Bannister (Mo. 2022, blank audio),
  United States v. Tate (C.A.A.F. 2021); Texas Rule of Appellate Procedure 34.6(f) grants
  a new trial when a significant recorded portion is lost or inaudible through no fault of
  the appellant.
- A Montgomery County, Texas trial court excluded a non-stenographic deposition
  transcript in a civil case (reported by Esquire on JD Supra, snippet only; no case name
  or reason found).
- **How often does this happen? Unknown.** NCRA's "Risk Your Record" list counts 12,923
  appellate cases matching a keyword search for words like "inaudible" (seen). That is a
  word count, not a count of failed recordings. The list itself admits of one case, "We
  suspect that the proceedings were electronically recorded." The Texas study says courts
  do not track it.

### Courts changing systems

**Toward stenographers (confirmed, but narrow)**
- **Florida**: since 2016, circuit plans must prohibit digital court reporting in
  death-penalty trials and post-conviction cases, to "ensure the accuracy of transcripts"
  (Florida Bar News, 15 Apr 2016, seen). No specific failure was cited.
- **Oregon**: SB 384 (2015) would have required a certified shorthand reporter in
  aggravated murder cases. It appears to have stalled in committee (legislature page, seen;
  not confirmed).
- **Hawaii**: the judiciary "brought back retired court reporters on a temporary basis" to
  clear a transcript backlog (Hawaii News Now, 18 Jun 2025, seen). That is about the
  backlog, not about giving up recording.

**Toward stenographers (claimed by advocacy groups, not confirmed)**
- An NCRA-journal article (Journal of Court Reporting, 22 Feb 2021, seen) says Texas
  "brought back stenographic reporters after trying both audio and video taping methods";
  "New Mexico started using recording systems in 1982. By 1986, the state brought back
  stenographic reporters"; Nevada "brought back stenographic reporters in 1995"; Illinois's
  video systems "sit idle". **I could not confirm any of these independently.** New
  Mexico today runs FTR in its courts (see the July 2026 policy above), so whatever
  happened in 1986 did not last.
- The Texas Court Reporters Association's template comment to the legislature (2021,
  seen; the same wording appears 5 times): "Many states that previously implemented ER in
  courts returned to court reporters ... lack of confidence in the system by attorneys who
  hired their own court reporters." No states are named.

**Toward recording, and stayed**
- **Kentucky** has used video records for about 30 years, with an estimated "$24 million
  per year" saved (Indiana Lawyer, 3 Jul 2012, seen). The same article quotes an Indiana
  appeals judge who thinks video "will slow both the briefing and decisional parts".
- **Massachusetts** eliminated official court reporter positions in 2018 (snippet only).
  **Utah** has relied "primarily on digital recording systems" since July 2009 (snippet
  only).
- **Nationally (Texas study, 2026)**: "Nineteen states operate under permissive statewide
  authorization, eight mandate DCR, twenty states use hybrid or conditional authorization,
  and only three states, including Texas, do not have an explicit statutory framework."
  Federal bankruptcy and magistrate courts use recording.
- **California**: the Los Angeles Superior Court authorised recording in civil, family and
  probate cases in Sept 2024 despite the statute (snippet only). In Aug 2026 the
  California Supreme Court held that low-income litigants must be given a verbatim record,
  by recording if no reporter is available. Chief Justice Guerrero: "a verbatim record
  created by electronic recording is better than no record at all." The lawyer for the
  court reporters' side called it "an inferior method that may be used only if court
  reporters are unavailable" (Daily Journal, 10 Aug 2026, seen by the research agent).

### Anecdotes

**Count: 10 first-hand accounts against recorded records, 1 in favour, 0 mixed.** Each
distinct person counts once. Only 2 of the 10 are about depositions with a digital
reporter; the rest are about courts recording their own proceedings.

Against:
1. Melissa B. Buchman, California family lawyer (Daily Journal, 13 Apr 2022; reprint
   hosted by NCRA's journal, seen). A "digital recorder" covered a remote deposition and
   the transcript left out 55 pages; her client lost a restraining-order request. Her
   rule since: "Never take a deposition or defend a deposition unless it is before a
   Certified Shorthand Reporter". **But** the agency's own email, quoted in her article,
   says the testimony "was originally written, but was errantly not merged into the
   transcript". So the failure was in putting the transcript together, not in capturing
   the audio. NCRA's 2023 white paper retells it as "all uncaptured by the digital
   recording" (seen), which goes further than the source. Her co-author is a former
   president of the California deposition reporters' association.
2. Brian Jasper, injury lawyer: a recorder lost power mid-deposition; "I have much more
   confidence in a stenographer because they are taking it down in real time" (Law360
   Pulse, 7 Apr 2022, seen by the research agent).
3. Darren Meade, defence lawyer: a transcript noted that much of his closing argument was
   unintelligible (same article, paraphrased).
4. Brewster Rawls, lawyer: transcript quality "very hit or miss" (same article; setting
   unclear).
5. Jennifer Gibo, lawyer, Paris, Texas: a full-day contested custody hearing was
   recorded, "there was some sort of issue with the recording", and "This led to us having
   to have additional hearings." (Public comment on Texas HB 228, 17 Mar 2021, seen.)
6. Daniel Young, criminal lawyer, Fort Worth: "recordings are never of good enough
   quality" (same docket; he seems to include police recordings).
7. Dick Harpootlian, defence counsel in the Murdaugh trial, called a digital firm's rough
   draft a "deficit product" (NCRA's journal, 13 Feb 2023, second-hand).
8. "Autumn", a blog commenter, describes a federal jury trial on a recording system as
   "a total disaster" with the sidebar missing (myShingle, comment undated; her role is not
   stated).
9. Dave Migliaccio, appellate lawyer: "The transcripts from recordings are invariably
   laden with 'inaudibles.' ... Give me reported over recorded any day." (same thread).
10. The separate opinion in State v. Hull, above.

In favour:
1. Retired Kentucky judge William L. Knopf, on video records replacing reporters'
   transcripts (published by JAVS, a vendor).

**How representative?** Not very. Almost all the negative accounts surfaced through
NCRA's journal, a legislative docket that court reporters organised (about 524 comments,
most from reporters), or a blog thread. Failures get written up; transcripts that go fine
do not. The only favourable first-hand account is published by a vendor. **Reddit could
not be checked at all**: both reddit.com and old.reddit.com refused the requests, so the
ordinary-lawyer forums the question pointed to are a blank, not evidence either way.

## 3. Who is saying what

| Source | Side | Note |
|---|---|---|
| FTR, Verbit, Rev, VIQ, JAVS, Esquire | Vendors, for digital/AI | Own words, no methods published for any figure |
| AAERT | Digital reporters' association, for | Sponsored by FTR; the "just as accurate" claim rests on a perception survey |
| STTI (Speech-to-Text Institute) | For | Verbit invested in it; funding otherwise not stated |
| NCRA, Journal of Court Reporting, state reporter associations | Court reporters, against | Supplies most of the failure stories and all of the "states went back" claims |
| Federal Judicial Center, NCSC, Texas OCA/PPRI, Judicial Council of California | Courts and researchers | Cost interest; the Texas study is the most balanced |
| Ashtabula County, New Mexico Judicial Branch | Buyers | Documents show what was actually bought and promised |
| Law360, ABA Journal, Daily Journal, Florida Bar News | Press | Mostly relay one side or the other |

**Both sides get things wrong.** FTR turned AAERT's "76% reporting that it creates
scheduling difficulties" into "76% of scheduling delays ... directly tied to the
shortage" (above). NCRA's 2023 white paper (seen) gives the Stanford "Race Gap" speech
recognition study as "about 20 percent for white male speakers, 40 percent for Black male
speakers, and 35 percent and 19 percent for white female and Black female speaker
respectively". The study itself (Koenecke et al., PNAS 2020) reports 0.35 for Black
speakers and 0.19 for white speakers overall; by group, 0.41 Black men, 0.30 Black women,
0.21 white men, 0.17 white women (per its abstract and press coverage). So NCRA attached
the race averages to the wrong groups. NCRA's retelling of the Buchman case is also
stronger than her own article.

**"Speak Up For Justice."** I could not find a court-reporter campaign by this name. The
only match was a Washington State Bar Association programme about threats to judges. It
may exist under a different name; not confirmed either way.

## 4. Looked for and not found

- Any FTR claim of 99%, "as accurate as a stenographer", "court-ready" or "no loss of
  record". The strongest is the unqualified 98%.
- Any FTR proposal or contract that commits to an accuracy figure. The method or test
  audio behind any FTR percentage.
- Any independent or court-run accuracy test of FTR RealTime or any other court AI
  product.
- A documented private sales pitch (email, phone, demo) with a specific accuracy claim.
  Only general, unsourced warnings from NCRA.
- Reseller or partner pages repeating FTR claims.
- Independent confirmation that New Mexico, Texas, Nevada or Illinois went back to
  stenographers after trying recording.
- Any lawyer saying they went back to stenographers after using digital reporters, apart
  from Buchman's personal rule.
- A named lawyer's first-hand good experience with a digital deposition reporter.
- A survey asking lawyers stenographer versus digital; any measured rate of "inaudible"
  in court transcripts; how often recording failures lead to reversals.
- Reddit (blocked), the original Murdaugh blog post, the 2021 Wyoming Lawyer article "The
  Downside of Digital Court Reporters" (page came back empty), STTI's own reports.
