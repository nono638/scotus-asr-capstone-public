Research notes for the capstone thesis: every transcript is one listener's judgement of what was said; some transcribers (human or machine) use context to resolve ambiguity, others stick to the literal utterance.

## 1. Classic arguments that transcription is theory-laden or political

### Takeaway
Since Ochs (1979), the linguistics and qualitative-methods literature has agreed that a transcript is not a neutral copy of speech. It is a selective, theory-driven and (Bucholtz 2000) politically consequential set of choices about *what* to write down (interpretation) and *how* to write it (representation). There is no "neutral" option: both the "cleaned-up" (naturalized/denaturalism) style and the "every hesitation" (denaturalized/naturalism) style serve some readers and goals and disadvantage others.

### Inferences
- The thesis ("every transcript is one listener's judgement") is the settled view of this literature, not a provocative claim. Ochs (1979), Bucholtz (2000, 2007), Green et al. (1997) and Oliver et al. (2005) all say it in different words. The capstone's new contribution is to apply it to **machine** transcribers and to compare them with court reporters on the same audio.
- The naturalized/denaturalized axis lines up with the ASR comparison. Engines that output clean, punctuated, disfluency-free text (Whisper-style) produce *naturalized* transcripts. Engines or reference sets that keep "uh", false starts and repetitions are closer to *denaturalized*. A word error rate (WER) gap can therefore reflect a difference in *transcription philosophy* rather than in hearing.
- Oliver et al.'s "annual"/"anal" case is a clean human example of "using context to resolve ambiguity". It works as an analogy for language-model-conditioned ASR.

## 2. Conversation-analysis conventions and how much detail is "accurate"

### Takeaway
Conversation analysis (CA) represents one end of the detail spectrum. Jefferson's notation records overlaps, timed pauses, stretched sounds, in-breaths and laughter particles because CA holds that these details carry meaning. Even so, CA authors accept that no transcript is complete. Applied work on legal transcripts shows that leaving such features out changes how readers judge a speaker.

### Cited Findings
- **Jefferson 2004 (book chapter):** Jefferson, Gail. 2004. "Glossary of transcript symbols with an introduction." In G. H. Lerner (ed.), *Conversation Analysis: Studies from the First Generation*, 13–31. Amsterdam: John Benjamins. [unverified this session] Conventions include `[ ]` for overlap, `(0.5)` for timed pauses, `(.)` for a micropause, `::` for sound stretch, `.hh` for in-breath, `=` for latching, and underlining for stress. Jefferson's introduction defends recording things such as laughter tokens on the grounds that they turn out to be interactionally organised. [unverified]
- **Haworth et al. 2023 (peer-reviewed article):** Haworth, Kate, James Tompkinson, Emma Richardson, Felicity Deamer & Magnus Hamann. 2023. "'For the Record': applying linguistics to improve evidential consistency in police investigative interview records." *Frontiers in Communication* 8: 1178516. DOI 10.3389/fcomm.2023.1178516. — [Frontiers](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/communication/articles/10.3389/fcomm.2023.1178516/full) [verified]
  - "the intention is not the production of a 'perfect' transcript, since this is an impossibility." [verified]
  - Conversion from speech to writing "is necessarily subjective and inexact." [verified]
  - "it is impossible for any transcriber not to bring in their own perspectives and unconscious biases." [verified]
  - Omitting emotion is "a subjective choice in itself, affecting the meaning conveyed." [verified]
  - Inconsistency in police records: "a series of dots ('…..') was used to indicate four different phenomena". Pauses were "either omitted, or transcribed inconsistently." [verified]
  - Their own experiments (Deamer et al. 2022; Tompkinson et al. 2023) found that people who **read** an interview transcript judged the interviewee as less credible and more likely to be lying than people who **heard** the audio. [verified, as reported in Haworth et al.]
- **Tompkinson & Haworth (2024)** examine whether police transcripts should represent pauses, overlapping talk, laughter and emphasis. — [White Rose eprint](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/234880/1/Tompkinson_Haworth.pdf) [verified: topic only]
- **Ochs 1979** argues that more detail is not automatically better: a transcript too dense to read fails its purpose, so the level of detail must follow the research question. [second-hand, via Oliver et al. 2005 and Davidson 2009 summaries] — [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1400594)

### Inferences
- "Accurate" is relative to purpose. A CA transcript, a court record, a sociolinguistic transcript and an ASR reference are each "accurate" for a different reader. WER against one kind of reference measures agreement with that reference's conventions, not agreement with "what was said."
- The Haworth/Deamer finding (readers judge the speaker more harshly than listeners do) is direct evidence that a transcript's format, not only its words, changes the outcome. This is useful for the court-reporter half of the paper.

## 3. Transcribing dialect: eye dialect, AAE, and court reporters

### Takeaway
Dialect spellings ("eye dialect", "wif dat") stigmatize speakers: readers take them as signs of low status (Preston 1985; Jaffe & Walton 2000). Standard spelling, on the other hand, can erase grammatical features that change meaning, such as AAE habitual *be*. Court reporters, certified on the speech of lawyers and judges, mis-transcribe ordinary AAE at high rates (Jones et al. 2019), and the Jeantel testimony shows what that costs in a real trial (Rickford & King 2016).

### Cited Findings

**Preston 1985 (peer-reviewed article)**
- Preston, Dennis R. 1985. "The Li'l Abner syndrome: Written representations of speech." *American Speech* 60(4): 328–336. — [course bibliography listing](https://personal.unizar.es/garciala/bibliography/Subjects/./4.Genres/Academic.writing/Acad.jour.CONTENTS/Am.Speech.docx); [Preston's ADS-L post on the date](https://americandialect.org/americandialectarchives/aug94067.html) [verified: citation; the 1985 vs 1986 date confusion is resolved by Preston himself as 1985]
- Preston "strongly opposes the use of such respellings in discourse, folklore, ethnography and other fields that represent the actual speech of respondents" (Preston's own 1994 ADS-L listserv post, which is informal, not peer reviewed). — [ADS-L archive](https://americandialect.org/americandialectarchives/aug94067.html) [second-hand summary of the post]
- Core argument (from memory, [unverified wording]): respellings such as "wuz" and "sez" show no real difference in pronunciation (everyone says /wʌz/). They only signal that the speaker is uneducated or rustic, so they denigrate speakers while adding no phonetic information.

**Jaffe & Walton 2000 (peer-reviewed article)**
- Jaffe, Alexandra & Shana Walton. 2000. "The voices people read: Orthography and the representation of non-standard speech." *Journal of Sociolinguistics* 4(4): 561–587. Special issue introduced by Jaffe 2000, pp. 497–513. — [search summary of abstract](https://benjamins.com/online/ebop/publications/15894) [verified: abstract-level]
- Using matched-guise tests, interviews and read-aloud tasks, they found that readers took nonstandard spellings as signs of low socioeconomic status, "uncritically and spontaneously". Readers' own oral performances shifted when reading standard versus nonstandard spellings. [abstract-level]
- The same special issue has Miethaner on transcribing Earlier African American English (pp. 534–560). [abstract-level]

**Jones, Kalbfeld, Hancock & Clark 2019 (peer-reviewed article)**
- Jones, Taylor, Jessica Rose Kalbfeld, Ryan Hancock & Robin Clark. 2019. - Abstract (summary): reporters are certified at 95% (or 98%) accuracy, but that test does not measure skill with nonstandard dialects. ### Inferences
- The bind for any transcriber of CORAAL-type speech:
  - Standard spelling (naturalized) erases features such as habitual *be*, copula absence and negative concord, or invites "correcting" them into Standard English. Jones et al.'s "he don't be" became "we going to be" shows the *meaning* changing.
  - Dialect respelling stigmatizes the speaker (Preston; Jaffe & Walton).
  - ASR trained on standard text faces the same bind, and its language model will "correct" toward Standard American English. This is the machine version of the reporters' errors.
- Jones et al. report that reporter race and experience did not predict accuracy. That supports framing the problem as *exposure and expectation*, not individual bias, which fits the "listener's judgement" thesis.

## 4. Forensic linguistics: the "verbatim" record as myth

### Takeaway
Forensic linguists have shown for four decades that the official legal transcript, though treated in law as identical to what was said, is an edited product shaped by reporters' conventions, and that appellate courts read only that product. Walker (1985 dissertation; 1986; 1990) is the foundational source. Eades, Tiersma, Coulthard and Haworth extend the critique to courtroom and police transcripts.

### Cited Findings
- **Walker 1990 (book chapter):** Walker, Anne Graffam. 1990. "Language at work in the law: The customs, conventions, and appellate consequences of court reporting." In J. N. Levi & A. G. Walker (eds.), *Language in the Judicial Process*, 203–244. New York: Plenum. DOI 10.1007/978-1-4899-3719-3_7. — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1007/978-1-4899-3719-3_7) [verified: title and abstract via search snippet; pages unverified]
  - Abstract (search snippet): the "necessary presumption" behind court reporting is that an accurate record of an oral event can be made by writing down exactly what was said. The chapter examines that presumption and the customs that modify it. [search snippet; not read in full]
  - From memory [unverified]: Walker surveyed and interviewed reporters. She found widespread routine editing: removing false starts, fillers and stutters, fixing grammar for judges and attorneys more than for witnesses, and choosing punctuation that changes meaning. - **Fraser (2014, 2018, 2022)** works on transcripts of indistinct covert recordings and on how police-supplied transcripts prime what juries hear. [second-hand titles via Haworth et al.] See section 5.

### Inferences
- Walker's main point fits the court-reporter half of the paper: the "verbatim" record is a professional *genre* with house conventions, not a recording. When the paper compares ASR against official Supreme Court transcripts, the reference is itself an edited genre. Some ASR "errors" will be cases where the engine was *more* literal than the reporter.
- The legal system's assumption that the transcript is "in essence identical" to the audio is what makes transcription errors consequential (appeals, the Jeantel case, police interviews).

## 5. Is unbiased transcription possible? Perception research

### Takeaway
No. Speech perception is partly top-down: listeners fill in missing sounds from context (Warren 1970). Expectations about who is speaking, including race seen in a photograph, change what listeners report hearing and how much they understand (Rubin 1992; Kang & Rubin 2009). In forensic settings, a suggested transcript primes listeners to hear it in ambiguous audio (Fraser). A transcriber, human or machine, always brings a model of what is likely to be said.

### Cited Findings
- **Warren 1970 (peer-reviewed article):** Warren, Richard M. 1970. "Perceptual restoration of missing speech sounds." *Science* 167(3917): 392–393. — [MIT course copy (1969 manuscript)](https://web.mit.edu/9.35/www/archives/2020/auditory/papers/Warren_PhonemicRestoration.pdf) [verified: citation and finding]
  - In "The state governors met with their respective legislatures convening in the capital city", the first /s/ of "legislatures" was replaced by a cough. Listeners reported hearing the full word.
  - They could not tell where the cough had occurred; half placed it in the wrong position.
  - A silent gap did not produce the illusion.
  — [Pressbooks textbook summary](https://ecampusontario.pressbooks.pub/essentialsoflinguistics2/part/chapter-13-psycholinguistics-and-neurolinguistics/phonemic-restoration/); [MIT copy](https://web.mit.edu/9.35/www/archives/2020/auditory/papers/Warren_PhonemicRestoration.pdf)
- **Rubin 1992 (peer-reviewed article):** Rubin, Donald L. 1992. "Nonlanguage factors affecting undergraduates' judgments of nonnative English-speaking teaching assistants." *Research in Higher Education* 33(4): 511–531. [unverified this session]
  - Design (described in reviews): the same lecture recorded by a native speaker of Standard American English was played with a photo of an Asian woman or a white woman.
  - Students who saw the Asian face rated the speaker as more accented, and their listening comprehension went down, though the voice was identical.
  — [review summary via search](https://www.scientia.global/wp-content/uploads/Kim_McDonough/Professor-Kim-McDonough_Professor-Masatoshi-Sato.pdf) [second-hand]
- **Kang & Rubin 2009 (peer-reviewed article):** Kang, Okim & Donald L. Rubin. 2009. "Reverse linguistic stereotyping: Measuring the effect of listener expectations on speech evaluation." *Journal of Language and Social Psychology* 28(4): 441–456. [unverified pages]
  - Names the process "reverse linguistic stereotyping": a listener's assumptions about a speaker's group membership distort how the speech is perceived and judged.
  - NAU coverage describes 150 undergraduates hearing an astronomy lecture paired with different photos. Those who saw an Asian face reported more difficulty and comprehended less.
  — [NAU news](https://news.nau.edu/?p=52088) [second-hand, university press]
- An Australian replication reported that students who saw an Asian woman's photo said they heard a "foreign" and "Asian" accent "despite this accent being completely absent in the auditory signal." [second-hand search snippet; the specific study was not identified]
- **Fraser (forensic phonetics, Helen Fraser):** her work shows that police-supplied transcripts of indistinct recordings prime listeners. Once primed, listeners "hear" the suggested words and find it hard to un-hear them. Cited in Haworth et al. 2023 (Fraser 2014, 2018, 2022). [second-hand titles; the experiments themselves were not read] Likely key paper: Fraser, Stevenson & Marks 2011, "Interpretation of a crisis call: Persistence of a primed perception of a disputed utterance," *International Journal of Speech, Language and the Law* 18(2). [unverified]
- Jones et al. 2019: reporters "assumed criminality on the part of the speakers, just from hearing how the speakers sounded." This shows listener expectation operating in professional transcription. — [languagejones.com](https://www.languagejones.com/blog-1/tag/justice)
- Oliver et al. 2005: "a transcriber hears the interview through his/her own cultural-linguistic filters." — [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1400594)

### Inferences
- Phonemic restoration is the human counterpart of an ASR language model. Both fill gaps with the most probable word given context. This is the mechanism behind "using context to resolve ambiguity", and also behind confident errors (Whisper-style hallucinated or "corrected" words).
- Reverse linguistic stereotyping suggests that human transcribers' accuracy on EdAcc-style accented speech or CORAAL could depend on what they *believe* about the speaker, not only on the signal. ASR has no face to look at, but its training data encodes a comparable prior (what "typical" speech is).
- So "unbiased transcription" is not possible. The best that can be done is a *documented* bias: stated conventions, audio kept linked to the text (Kendall), and reflexive disclosure (Bucholtz 2000; Davidson 2009).

### Gaps
- Rubin 1992 and Kang & Rubin 2009 effect sizes and procedures were not read in the original.
- I found no study that tests transcription accuracy itself (as opposed to comprehension or accent ratings) against speaker race primed by photograph. That would be the closest analogue to court reporting.
