# History of recording speech in writing: scribes, shorthand writers, early reporters, and the "words vs. sense" question

Research notes, 3 November 2026. Source types are flagged in square brackets: [PRIMARY: edition/translation], [PEER-REVIEWED], [BOOK], [ENCYCLOPEDIA], [POPULAR], [BLOG/LISTSERV], [OFFICIAL]. Where a translation is my own literal gloss rather than a published one, it says "my gloss". Primary texts were downloaded and checked word for word (Project Gutenberg, LacusCurtius, Wikisource, The Latin Library, PerseusDL GitHub XML).

## Q0. Overall: did ancient and early modern writers already argue about what a "true" record of speech is?

### Takeaway
Yes, and they set out both positions. Thucydides (c. 400 BC) said outright that word-for-word recall was impossible and that he kept to "the general sense". Roman shorthand writers (*notarii*, *exceptores*) produced word-level records, and these sat alongside polished versions the speakers published themselves: Cicero's *Pro Milone* survived in both a stenographic copy and a rewritten one. The Conference of Carthage (AD 411) set up a cross-checked, speaker-approved transcript to guarantee accuracy. Eighteenth-century court shorthand writers openly took down "the substance" and left out false starts. The 1907 Hansard definition turned the compromise into a rule: "substantially the verbatim report". The tension a court reporter sees between ASR (every token) and an edited record (the sense) is at least 2,400 years old.

### Cited Findings
- Thucydides 1.22.1: it was "difficult to carry them word for word in one's memory", so he made speakers say what the occasion demanded, "adhering as closely as possible to the general sense of what they really said." — [Crawley trans., Project Gutenberg #7142](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/7142/pg7142.txt) [PRIMARY]
- Asconius (1st c. AD) says the shorthand copy of Cicero's delivered *Pro Milone* still survived ("Manet autem illa quoque excepta eius oratio") alongside the polished version "which we read". — [Asconius, In Milonianam, Latin Library](https://www.thelatinlibrary.com/asconius.html) [PRIMARY]
- At Carthage in 411, "Both sides had a team of speedwriters, and at the end of the sessions of the collatio, these stenographers gathered, compared their notes and agreed on a final text, which in turn had to be approved by the recorded speakers." — [Riedlberger, review of Weidmann ed., Plekos 22 (2020)](https://www.plekos.uni-muenchen.de/2020/r-weidmann.pdf) [PEER-REVIEWED review]
- Thomas Gurney, Old Bailey shorthand writer, at the 1754 Canning trial: "It is my method, if a question brings out an imperfect answer, and is obliged to be asked over again, and the answer comes more strong, I take that down as the proper evidence, and neglect the other. . . . It is not to be expected I should write every unintelligible word" — quoted in [Shoemaker, JBS 47 (2008), White Rose eprint](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/4697/1/shoemakerr2.pdf) [PEER-REVIEWED, quoting PRIMARY]
- House of Commons, 14 May 1908 (Hobhouse): a "full" report "means a verbatim report, trimmed of all those excrescences and redundancies with which Members are perhaps in the habit of filling up the matter of their speeches." — [HC Deb 14 May 1908 vol 188 cc1357-8](https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/commons/1908/may/14/class-ii) [PRIMARY/OFFICIAL]

### Inferences
- There are two traditions. One is "sense" (Thucydides, the Old Bailey "abstracts", Hansard's "full report"). The other is "word" (the Roman *exceptores*, the Carthage protocol with its disfluencies left in, the 1778 demand for a "true, fair, and perfect narrative"). Which one won depended on the record's purpose: history or persuasion, versus legal proof.
- Ancient writers already noticed that the speaker could revise the record after the event (Cicero), which is the ancestor of modern "errata"/read-and-sign review of a deposition.

### Gaps
- No single ancient text defines a "true transcript" in the abstract. The debate is reconstructed from practice and asides.

## Q1. Ancient Greece: tachygraphy, Xenophon, Thucydides 1.22

### Takeaway
Thucydides 1.22 is the classic and best-attested statement that the sense is preferred to the exact words. Xenophon's "shorthand" is a later interpretation of a vague Greek verb in Diogenes Laertius and should be called a legend or disputed claim. I could not verify the Acropolis "shorthand stone" online in this session.

### Cited Findings
**Thucydides 1.22.1 (c. 400 BC)**
- Full Crawley translation (1874): "With reference to the speeches in this history, some were delivered before the war began, others while it was going on; some I heard myself, others I got from various quarters; it was in all cases difficult to carry them word for word in one's memory, so my habit has been to make the speakers say what was in my opinion demanded of them by the various occasions, of course adhering as closely as possible to the general sense of what they really said." — [Thucydides, trans. Richard Crawley, Project Gutenberg #7142](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/7142/pg7142.txt) [PRIMARY; Crawley 1874, public domain]
- Greek text (Oxford text by Jones/Powell as encoded by Perseus): "χαλεπὸν τὴν ἀκρίβειαν αὐτὴν τῶν λεχθέντων διαμνημονεῦσαι ἦν … ὡς δ' ἂν ἐδόκουν ἐμοὶ ἕκαστοι περὶ τῶν αἰεὶ παρόντων τὰ δέοντα μάλιστ' εἰπεῖν, ἐχομένῳ ὅτι ἐγγύτατα τῆς ξυμπάσης γνώμης τῶν ἀληθῶς λεχθέντων, οὕτως εἴρηται." — [PerseusDL canonical-greekLit tlg0003.tlg001.perseus-grc2.xml](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/PerseusDL/canonical-greekLit/master/data/tlg0003/tlg001/tlg0003.tlg001.perseus-grc2.xml) [PRIMARY]
  - Key words (my gloss): *tēn akribeian autēn tōn lechthentōn* = "the exact precision itself of the things said". This is the *verbatim* ideal, which Thucydides says he could not reach. *tēs xumpasēs gnōmēs tōn alēthōs lechthentōn* = "the overall intent/gist of what was truly said". This is the *sense* standard he adopted. The paper can line up *akribeia* against *gnōmē* as the ancient form of the verbatim vs. meaning split.
- In the same chapter he applies a stricter test to events than to speeches: "the accuracy of the report being always tried by the most severe and detailed tests possible," and he notes discrepancies between eye-witnesses "arising sometimes from imperfect memory, sometimes from undue partiality for one side or the other." — [Crawley trans.](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/7142/pg7142.txt) [PRIMARY]
- Other translations to cite: Rex Warner (Penguin, 1954), C. F. Smith (Loeb, 1919), Martin Hammond (Oxford World's Classics, 2009). Not checked in this session. The Crawley wording quoted above is the one that matches the user's phrase "general sense".

**Xenophon and Diogenes Laertius 2.48 (3rd c. AD)**
- Hicks (Loeb 1925) translation: "He was the first to take notes of, and to give to the world, the conversation of Socrates, under the title of Memorabilia." — [Diogenes Laertius, Lives of Eminent Philosophers II, trans. R. D. Hicks, Wikisource](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Lives_of_the_Eminent_Philosophers/Book_II) [PRIMARY; Loeb 1925]
- Greek: "καὶ πρῶτος ὑποσημειωσάμενος τὰ λεγόμενα εἰς ἀνθρώπους ἤγαγεν, Ἀπομνημονεύματα ἐπιγράψας." — [PerseusDL tlg0004.tlg001.perseus-grc2.xml](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/PerseusDL/canonical-greekLit/master/data/tlg0004/tlg001/tlg0004.tlg001.perseus-grc2.xml) [PRIMARY]
  - My gloss: *hyposēmeiōsamenos* = "having noted down / jotted notes of". The verb is related to *sēmeion*, "sign", which is why some later writers read it as "shorthand", but it does not mean that in itself. Hicks translates it simply as "take notes of".
- Popular sources run with the "Xenophon invented tachygraphy" idea (e.g., a 2026 *Greek Reporter* piece titled "Is Xenophon the Founder of Tachygraphy?"), but by the search summary's own account it offers no early inscription evidence. — [Greek Reporter, 7 Mar 2026](https://greekreporter.com/2026/03/07/xenophon-founder-tachygraphy/) [POPULAR; treat as legend]
- A 1901 *Journal of Hellenic Studies* article ("On Old Greek Tachygraphy") says the existence of a Greek system parallel to Roman Tironian notes is hard to prove, and asks whether early Greek tachygraphy was real speed-writing or mainly secret or space-saving writing. — [JHS, Cambridge Core](https://cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-hellenic-studies/article/on-old-greek-tachygraphy/22F46C5921AE6CF075B8596B4B540BC3) [PEER-REVIEWED, old; abstract only seen]
- Greek shorthand is well attested later, in papyri from Roman Egypt (1st–7th c. AD), including manuals and marginalia. Libanius (4th c. AD) complained that students in Antioch chased shorthand at the expense of rhetoric. — [McNamee, GRBS (Duke)](https://grbs.library.duke.edu/index.php/grbs/article/download/1981/3181/12121) [PEER-REVIEWED; via search summary]

### Inferences
- In a paper, Xenophon should be presented as "a note-taker of Socrates' conversations, according to a late (3rd c. AD) biographer". The "shorthand" reading rests on one ambiguous verb and is a later embellishment.
- *Memorabilia* (ἀπομνημονεύματα) literally means "recollections", which points to memory-based reconstruction, Thucydides' method again, rather than word-level capture.

### Gaps
- **Acropolis stone:** I could not confirm online the 4th-century BC Acropolis marble fragment usually cited as the earliest Greek shorthand/abbreviation system. From memory it is usually given as IG II² 2783, c. mid-4th c. BC, discussed by Milne (*Greek Shorthand Manuals*, 1934) and Boge (*Griechische Tachygraphie und Tironische Noten*, 1973). Searches for "IG II2 2783" returned nothing relevant. **Verify in IG or in Boge before citing.** Scholars debate whether it is a speed-writing system or just abbreviation.
- Boge, *Griechische Tachygraphie* (1973) was not reachable online.

## Q2. Rome: Tiro, Cato's speech (Plutarch), Pro Milone, Seneca, Martial, notarii/exceptores, Carthage 411

### Takeaway
Rome supplies the best "words vs. sense" anecdotes. Cicero had Cato's speech taken down by trained shorthand clerks (Plutarch). Cicero's delivered *Pro Milone* survived in a stenographic copy *and* in a rewritten published version, and Milo joked about the difference (Dio, Asconius). Seneca and Martial show shorthand as a fast, low-status, slave skill. The Conference of Carthage in 411 is the strongest ancient example of a formally verified transcript: rival stenographic teams reconciled their notes and the speakers approved the text. The surviving text even keeps a bishop's false starts.

### Cited Findings
**Plutarch, *Cato the Younger* 23.3: Cato's speech on the Catilinarians, 5 Dec 63 BC**
- "This is the only speech of Cato which has been preserved, we are told, and its preservation was due to Cicero the consul, who had previously given to those clerks who excelled in rapid writing instruction in the use of signs, which, in small and short figures, comprised the force of many letters; these clerks he had then distributed in various parts of the senate-house. For up to that time the Romans did not employ or even possess what are called shorthand writers, but then for the first time, we are told, the first steps toward the practice were taken." — [Plutarch, Life of Cato Minor, Loeb vol. VIII (1919), LacusCurtius](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Plutarch/Lives/Cato_Minor*.html) [PRIMARY; Loeb trans. Bernadotte Perrin, 1919. The page does not name the translator, but Loeb Plutarch Lives are Perrin's]
- Note the hedges: Plutarch twice says "we are told" (λέγουσι), so this is reported tradition, not first-hand. Note also the multiple placement ("distributed in various parts of the senate-house"): several writers in different positions, an early form of redundant capture.

**Cicero's *Pro Milone* (52 BC): delivered vs. published version**
- Cassius Dio 40.54.2–3: Cicero, seeing Pompey and the soldiers in the court, "was alarmed and overwhelmed with dread, so that he did not deliver the speech he had prepared at all, but after uttering with difficulty a few words that all but died on his lips, was glad to retire. The speech which is now extant, purporting to have been delivered at that time in behalf of Milo, he wrote some time later and at leisure, when he had recovered his courage. … When Milo, in banishment, had read the speech sent to him by Cicero, he wrote back that it was lucky for him those words had not been spoken in that form in the court; for he should not be eating such mullets in Massilia (where he was passing his exile), if any such defence had been made." — [Cassius Dio, Roman History 40, Loeb vol. III (1914), LacusCurtius](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Cassius_Dio/40*.html) [PRIMARY; Loeb trans. Earnest Cary. Dio wrote c. AD 200, about 250 years after the event, so the anecdote is late]
- Asconius, commentary on *Pro Milone* (written c. AD 54–57, the earlier and better source): "Cicero cum inciperet dicere, exceptus est acclamatione Clodianorum … Itaque non ea qua solitus erat constantia dixit. Manet autem illa quoque excepta eius oratio: scripsit vero hanc quam legimus ita perfecte ut iure prima haberi possit." — [Asconius, Latin Library](https://www.thelatinlibrary.com/asconius.html) [PRIMARY; Clark's Oxford text, p. 41–42 C]
  - My gloss: "When Cicero began to speak, he was met by shouting from Clodius's supporters … so he did not speak with his usual firmness. That speech of his, *as taken down* [*excepta*], also survives; but the one we read he wrote so perfectly that it can rightly be considered his best." *Excipere* is the technical verb for stenographic capture (compare Seneca below and the job title *exceptor*). Asconius plays on it: Cicero was "taken up" (*exceptus*) by heckling, and his speech was "taken down" (*excepta*).
  - So in the 1st century AD there were **two texts of one speech**: the stenographic record of what was actually said, and the author's improved version. Readers (and Milo) knew they differed. This is a ready-made ancient parallel for "verbatim record vs. corrected/edited transcript".

**Seneca, *Epistles* 90.25 (c. AD 64)**
- Latin: "quid verborum notas quibus quamvis citata excipitur oratio et celeritatem linguae manus sequitur? Vilissimorum mancipiorum ista commenta sunt." — [Seneca, Ep. 90, Latin Library](https://www.thelatinlibrary.com/sen/seneca.ep14-15.shtml) [PRIMARY]
- Gummere (Loeb, 1920) translation: "Or our signs for whole words, which enable us to take down a speech, however rapidly uttered, matching speed of tongue by speed of hand? All this sort of thing has been devised by the lowest grade of slaves." — [Seneca, Moral Letters 90, trans. R. M. Gummere, Wikisource](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Moral_letters_to_Lucilius/Letter_90) [PRIMARY; Loeb]
- Gummere's footnote 17 (an editorial note, not Seneca): "Suetonius tells us that a certain Ennius, a grammarian of the Augustan age, was the first to develop shorthand on a scientific basis, and that Tiro, Cicero's freedman, had invented the process. He also mentions Seneca as the most scientific and encyclopaedic authority on the subject." — [same](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Moral_letters_to_Lucilius/Letter_90) [editorial note; the Suetonius material survives via Isidore, *Etymologies* 1.22, which I did not retrieve in this session]
- Use: Seneca treats verbatim capture as mechanical, slave work, *not* wisdom. This is an ancient form of the "mere transcription vs. understanding" hierarchy, useful when contrasting ASR (mechanical capture) with a reporter's judgement.

**Martial 14.208, "Notarius" (*Apophoreta*, late 1st c. AD)**
- Latin: "Currant verba licet, manus est velocior illis: / Nondum lingua suum, dextra peregit opus." — [Martial, Book 14, Latin Library](https://www.thelatinlibrary.com/martial/mart14.shtml) [PRIMARY]
- My literal gloss: "Though the words race, the hand is swifter than they: the tongue has not yet finished its work, and the right hand has already finished its own." (The Loeb translation is by Shackleton Bailey, 1993; not checked here.) This is a gift-tag poem, presumably for a slave stenographer given as a present. It is a good epigraph for a paper on transcription speed and latency.

**Conference of Carthage, AD 411 (*Gesta collationis Carthaginiensis*)**
- Riedlberger (review of Weidmann's CSEL 104 edition): "The whole event was minuted; this was nothing out of the ordinary, of course, but the employed procedure of stenographing was highly unusual. Both sides had a team of speedwriters, and at the end of the sessions of the collatio, these stenographers gathered, compared their notes and agreed on a final text, which in turn had to be approved by the recorded speakers. Nobody would be able to doubt the veracity of the resulting protocol, proving once and for all that the prevailing side was, indeed, the Catholic one." — [Peter Riedlberger, Plekos 22 (2020), 315ff.](https://www.plekos.uni-muenchen.de/2020/r-weidmann.pdf) [PEER-REVIEWED review]
- The same review says the official minutes themselves are lost: "The 'official' minutes are not extant, but we do have three texts derived from it". These are Augustine's *Breviculus collationis* (an abstract Augustine wrote because the full acts were too long and tedious to read), *Ad Donatistas post collationem*, and the *Gesta* (partial). — [Riedlberger 2020](https://www.plekos.uni-muenchen.de/2020/r-weidmann.pdf)
- **Disfluencies preserved.** "any neat copy of minutes is smoothed, at least up to a certain degree. But the transmitted text of the Gesta includes many oral features that might be considered 'mistakes' (cf. Weidmann, 22-23). The most stunning observation pertains to the repeated self-corrections of one specific person, Petilianus … [who] started to formulate a prepositional attribute, just in time noticed the colloquiality of it, and quickly substituted the genitive required in standard Latin; as this occurs predominantly with him, this must go back to the minutes themselves". — [Riedlberger 2020](https://www.plekos.uni-muenchen.de/2020/r-weidmann.pdf). Weidmann also keeps variant spellings of place names that may reflect individual speakers' or stenographers' habits. — [same]
- A Russian-language review of the same edition says four *custodes chartarum* (guardians of the records) were appointed to check the correctness of the records. — [patristic.ru review, via search summary](https://www.patristic.ru/jour/article/download/156/154) [PEER-REVIEWED review; I could not open the full text, so the details are not verified]
- Editions: Lancel, *Gesta conlationis Carthaginiensis anno 411*, CCSL 149A (Brepols, 1974) — [Brepols](https://www.brepols.net/products/IS-9782503014937-1); Weidmann, *Collatio Carthaginensis anni 411*, CSEL 104 (De Gruyter, 2018) — [De Gruyter](https://www.degruyterbrill.com/document/doi/10.1515/9783110471564/html); Weidmann, "Recording and reporting the Gesta Collationis Carthaginensis: problems and solutions", in *The Uniquely African Controversy* (2015), 85–100 — [Finding Augustine record](https://findingaugustine.org/Record/76610). English translation: Hermanowicz & McLynn, *The Conference of Carthage in 411* (Liverpool UP, Translated Texts for Historians) — [Blackwell's listing](https://blackwells.co.uk/bookshop/product/The-Conference-of-Carthage-in-411-by-Erika-Hermanowicz-Neil-McLynn/9781836243410) [BOOK; not consulted]

### Inferences
- Carthage 411 is the closest ancient analogue to a modern certified transcript: independent capture by opposing parties' stenographers, reconciliation, sign-off by the speakers, and a custodial chain. The fact that a speaker's grammatical self-repairs survived suggests that record leaned toward *verbatim*, unlike Hansard-style "cleaning".
- Pairing Asconius with Dio shows ancient readers knew a "record of what was said" and an "authorised text" can differ. Milo's mullet joke is a vivid way to say that the edited version would have changed the outcome.

### Gaps
- Teitler, *Notarii and Exceptores* (Amsterdam, 1985), the standard monograph on late-Roman stenographers, was not accessed. I can't quote it.
- Exact procedural citations within the *Gesta* (book.chapter for the edict on stenographers, *custodes chartarum*, and speakers' subscriptions) are unverified. Check Lancel/Weidmann, or the Hermanowicz–McLynn translation.
- Tiro and Tironian notes: the primary statements (Isidore, *Etym.* 1.22; Plutarch; Cicero's letters to Tiro) were not retrieved word for word in this session, apart from Gummere's note.
- I found no fetched primary source for "notarii in church councils" beyond Carthage.

## Q3. Medieval and religious: Augustine's sermons, scribal correction, Inquisition records (Fournier register / Montaillou)

### Takeaway
Augustine's sermons survive because hearers' stenographers took them down live. Only some were revised by him, so scholars still argue how faithful they are. The Fournier inquisition register (1318–25) is the classic case of a "transcript" that is really a translation and a reworking. Peasants spoke Occitan; scribes made quick notes, expanded them into Latin minutes with the inquisitor, and then read them back for correction. Historians (Herlihy, Boyle, Hay) criticised Le Roy Ladurie for treating them as the peasants' own voices.

### Cited Findings
**Augustine's sermons (late 4th–early 5th c.)**
- Possidius (Augustine's biographer) is quoted as saying: "Everyone employed stenographers, as much as he wanted and was able to, so that what Augustine said was retained in written form"; almost from the start of his preaching, his sermons were recorded by private stenographers and circulated. — [van Oort, HTS Teologiese Studies 65(1), SciELO PDF](https://www.scielo.org.za/pdf/hts/v65n1/50.pdf) [PEER-REVIEWED; via search summary, not opened in full]
- Most *Sermones ad populum* were taken down stenographically during preaching, but only some were revised by Augustine for publication. Recent scholarship names "the accuracy of stenographic records" and Augustine's own editing as method problems when using the sermons as evidence. — [search summary of Van Oort and others (UCLouvain/Pauliat, findingaugustine)](https://findingaugustine.org/Record/80648) [PEER-REVIEWED abstracts; secondary summary]

**Fournier Register / Montaillou (Pamiers, 1318–1325; Vat. Lat. 4030)**
- Process: during questioning "a scribe would make quick notes in short form to record the conversation. These would then be expanded into full minutes, which were then presented to the accused" for review and correction. A final version was then entered. The process "also involved translating the dialogue from the local Occitan to the Latin of the Church." — [Wikipedia, Fournier Register](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fournier_Register) [ENCYCLOPEDIA; use only as a pointer to Duvernoy/Le Roy Ladurie]
- Le Roy Ladurie's *Montaillou* (1975) was written from Jean Duvernoy's modern French translation (1965, 3 vols.), not directly from the Latin. So English readers get Occitan → Latin (scribe) → French (Duvernoy) → English (Bray), four languages deep. — [Wikipedia, Fournier Register / Montaillou (book)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Montaillou_(book)) [ENCYCLOPEDIA]
- David Herlihy criticised the translations in *Montaillou* as "often paraphrased, sometimes misleadingly". Cynthia Hay raised the problem of oral testimony given years after events; Le Roy Ladurie's use of the register "has been challenged as being insufficiently critical." — [Wikipedia, Montaillou (book), citing reviews](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Montaillou_(book)) [ENCYCLOPEDIA pointing to reviews]

### Inferences
- The Fournier register is a clean case study for "a transcript is an interpretation". Translation, compression, the questioner's framing and later read-back all come between the spoken word and the record. This is the same issue as a court interpreter's English rendering being the official record.
- The read-back step in the register is an early form of the witness-review procedure also seen at Carthage.

### Gaps
- Leonard E. Boyle, "Montaillou Revisited: *Mentalité* and Methodology" (in J. A. Raftis, ed., *Pathways to Medieval Peasants*, 1981). From memory this is the key methodological critique, arguing that the inquisitorial process shaped the testimony. I could not access it to quote.
- Carlo Ginzburg, "The Inquisitor as Anthropologist" (in *Clues, Myths, and the Historical Method*, 1989), was not accessed.
- Medieval scribal "correction" of texts (*correctores*, collation) was not researched in this pass.

## Q4. Early modern: Bright, shorthand in trial reporting, the Old Bailey Proceedings, Johnson, Dickens, Pitman

### Takeaway
English shorthand (Bright 1588, Shelton, later Gurney) spread first for sermon note-taking and then for trial and parliamentary reporting. Contemporaries already complained about fidelity. Heywood (1630s) said pirates took his play down "by stenography … scarce one word true". Wilkes (1775) and the judges objected to inaccurate Old Bailey reports. The official writer Thomas Gurney admitted he took down "the substance" and dropped imperfect answers. Modern scholarship (Langbein, Shoemaker, Devereaux, Huber) concludes the Proceedings are accurate in what they contain but heavily selective. Samuel Johnson's "parliamentary debates" were largely invented, and he later regretted that readers took them for genuine.

### Cited Findings
**Shorthand and sermons / plays (1588–1640s)**
- Bright's 1588 *Characterie* is the system known to have been used to take down sermons. For "well over half a century" scholars judged it incapable of producing the Shakespeare "bad quartos", though a more recent article argues *Romeo and Juliet* Q1 shows traces of shorthand origin. — [search summary: Project MUSE article](https://muse.jhu.edu/article/605066/summary); [Wikipedia, Timothie Bright](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Timothie_Bright) [PEER-REVIEWED abstract + ENCYCLOPEDIA]
- Thomas Heywood, prologue to *If You Know Not Me, You Know Nobody* (printed in *Pleasant Dialogues and Dramas*, 1637; revival c. 1633): "Some by stenography drew / The plot: put it in print: (scarce one word true)". — [Early Theatre (McMaster) article](https://prod-mulpress.lib.mcmaster.ca/earlytheatre/article/view/1203/2329) [PEER-REVIEWED; quote via search summary]. W. P. Williams argues from a full collation that Heywood's complaint is "substantially true"; Greg was more sceptical. — [same] This is an early-modern author's direct statement that a shorthand record can be unfaithful.
- Comenius (Jan Amos Komenský), visiting London in 1641, remarked on the many men and youths taking shorthand notes of sermons, which he saw as distinctly English. Shelton's system (used by Pepys) had special symbols for sermon note-takers. — [Kate Loveman, *The Strange History of Samuel Pepys's Diary* (CUP, 2025), "Shorthand and Secrecy" chapter, via search summary](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/strange-history-of-samuel-pepyss-diary/A9F3EB90492467E6EA9E669F92716E8D); [Loveman, Shelton extracts (Leicester)](https://pepyshistory.le.ac.uk/wp-content/uploads/2025/06/Extracts-from-Sheltons-shorthand-manual.pdf) [BOOK, university press]
- Kelly McCay, "A Writing by Strange Markes" (Harvard PhD diss., 2024) covers the invention and spread of English shorthand 1588–1700. — [search summary](https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/identifier/9781009554107%23BP2/type/BOOK_PART) [DISSERTATION; not consulted]

**Putney Debates (1647): shorthand in political record-keeping**
- Frances Henderson, "Reading, and writing, the text of the Putney Debates", in Michael Mendle (ed.), *The Putney Debates of 1647* (Cambridge UP, 2001): the standard study of William Clarke's shorthand record. — [search confirmation](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/historical-journal/article/what-really-happened-at-the-whitehall-debates-a-new-source/93B7E4AC10CDD705D7935374EEA3AA70) [BOOK chapter; not read]
- Carolyn Polizzotto (Historical Journal, 2014) found "a variant transcription of one of the Whitehall Debates" (14 Dec 1648) among the Clarke papers. Clarke's fair copy "was previously believed to be the only surviving record"; the variant "provides additional text, clarifies obscure passages". — [Polizzotto, "What really happened at the Whitehall Debates? A new source", Historical Journal](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/historical-journal/article/abs/what-really-happened-at-the-whitehall-debates-a-new-source/93B7E4AC10CDD705D7935374EEA3AA70) [PEER-REVIEWED]. This is a concrete case where two contemporary shorthand-derived transcripts of the same meeting differ.

**Old Bailey Proceedings (1674–1913)**
- 1720s: the Proceedings "first began to present trials as verbatim transcripts (rather than simply summarizing each trial)". — [Shoemaker, "The Old Bailey Proceedings and the Representation of Crime and Criminal Justice in Eighteenth-Century London", *Journal of British Studies* 47 (2008): 559–80, DOI 10.1086/587722](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/4697/1/shoemakerr2.pdf) [PEER-REVIEWED]
- Scale of omission: Richard Savage's 1727 murder trial "lasted eight hours, but the account in the Proceedings, partly presented in the first person as verbatim testimony, is only 2,447 words, which could easily have been spoken in under an hour." — [Shoemaker 2008](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/4697/1/shoemakerr2.pdf)
- John Langbein (quoted by Shoemaker): even in the 1780s, "when the sessions papers achieved their greatest detail, they were still omitting most of what was said at most of the trials they reported"; "the greatest shortcoming of the sessions papers as historical sources is their tendency to compress the trials they report," but "we need not worry about fabrication or invention of content." Shoemaker presents "a less sanguine view", citing "linguistic analysis that demonstrates the limitations of the shorthand system of note taking" (Magnus Huber 2007). — [Shoemaker 2008](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/4697/1/shoemakerr2.pdf)
- Normal practice was "abstracted" reporting: "the shorthand writer, knowing that not everything would be published, did not take down a full transcript of the evidence." Questions were often removed and answers merged into narrative. The publisher's note of September 1742 says questions were "omitted for brevity's sake". — [Shoemaker 2008](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/4697/1/shoemakerr2.pdf)
- **Thomas Gurney (official shorthand writer from 1748), Canning perjury trial, 1754.** Asked to confirm earlier evidence, he relied on his "minutes" for "the substance of . . . the evidence she gave in court", and explained: "It is my method, if a question brings out an imperfect answer, and is obliged to be asked over again, and the answer comes more strong, I take that down as the proper evidence, and neglect the other. . . . It is not to be expected I should write every unintelligible word". Public interest meant the publisher hired three shorthand writers, including Gurney, so that "every word" was taken for a separate 205-page book. — [Shoemaker 2008, citing *The Trial of Elizabeth Canning* (London, 1754), preface, 20, 104](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/4697/1/shoemakerr2.pdf). **This is the best early-modern quote for a court-reporter paper.**
- Gurney advertised his *Brachygraphy* (1750) in the Proceedings as "writer of these proceedings", which gave him "a clear motive to provide accurate transcriptions"; but "It was the printers, however, who determined the final content". — [Shoemaker 2008](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/4697/1/shoemakerr2.pdf)
- John Wilkes, Court of Common Council, Nov 1775: "the inaccurate manner in which the trials of the prisoners at the Old Bailey were delivered to the public, had been long and universally complained of; that the judges had often mentioned to him in his mayoralty that grievance; that the short-hand writer had hitherto been furnished with a power of publishing those trials without any control or revision; and that the learned and humane judge who tried the Perreaus had complained to him of the inexplicit manner in which those trials had been taken down, and that many parts of them were contrary to his own notes." — [Shoemaker 2008, quoting *Middlesex Journal*, 16–18 Nov 1775](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/4697/1/shoemakerr2.pdf). Here a judge checks a shorthand record against his own notes, which is an 18th-century accuracy audit.
- 1778, Committee for City Lands: the Proceedings must "contain a true, fair, and perfect narrative of the whole evidence upon the trial of every prisoner, whether he or she shall be convicted or acquitted"; trial accounts then got much longer (Devereaux). — [Shoemaker 2008, citing Journal of the Committee for City Lands, vol. 70, fol. 142v (13 Nov 1778), LMA; Devereaux, *Criminal Justice History* 18 (2002)](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/4697/1/shoemakerr2.pdf). Note the wording "true, fair, and perfect", close to the modern reporter's certificate ("true and accurate").
- Selectivity favoured the prosecution: in Catherine Hayes's case, prosecution testimony was given as verbatim while her defence was summarised "with some crucial testimony left out", as shown by comparison with *Select Trials*. — [Shoemaker 2008](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/4697/1/shoemakerr2.pdf)
- The Old Bailey Online's own page, Hitchcock & Shoemaker, "The Value of the Proceedings as a Historical Source", is cited by Shoemaker. The site returned HTTP 403 to my fetch, so it was not quoted. — [oldbaileyonline.org](https://www.oldbaileyonline.org/about/proceedings) [OFFICIAL project site; not retrieved]

**Samuel Johnson and the "Senate of Lilliput" (1740–43)**
- Boswell: Johnson wrote "the Debates in both houses of Parliament, under the name of 'The Senate of Lilliput,' sometimes with feigned denominations of the several speakers … Parliament then kept the press in a kind of mysterious awe, which made it necessary to have recourse to such devices." Sometimes Johnson "had nothing more communicated to him than the names of the several speakers, and the part which they had taken in the debate." — [Boswell, *Life of Johnson*, ed. C. G. Osgood (abridged, 1917), Project Gutenberg #1564](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/1564/pg1564.txt) [PRIMARY, abridged edition]
- Johnson later said "as soon as he found that the speeches were thought genuine he determined that he would write no more of them: for 'he would not be accessary to the propagation of falsehood.' And such was the tenderness of his conscience, that a short time before his death he expressed his regret for his having been the authour of fictions which had passed for realities." — [same, editorial note in the Osgood edition](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/1564/pg1564.txt) [PRIMARY-derived; the note originates with Johnson's early biographers (Hawkins/Nichols). Confirm the original before citing]
- The garret anecdote: when a speech of Pitt's in the *Gentleman's Magazine* was praised at a dinner, Johnson said he had written it in a garret in Exeter Street. The source is Arthur Murphy's *Essay on the Life and Genius of Samuel Johnson* (1792), so it is second-hand, and the details vary. A note in an edition of Boswell points out Johnson was not yet working for the magazine while living in Exeter Street. — [Grub Street Project, Exeter Street](https://www.grubstreetproject.net/places/34137/); [Wikisource page of Boswell's Life note](https://kwx.liehendr.com/content/wikisource_en_all_maxi_2022-09/A/Page%3ABoswell_-_Life_of_Johnson.djvu/619) [secondary; anecdote]
- Standard study: Benjamin Beard Hoover, *Samuel Johnson's Parliamentary Reporting: Debates in the Senate of Lilliput* (University of California Press, 1953). — [UC Press](https://www.ucpress.edu/books/samuel-johnsons-parliamentary-reporting/paper) [BOOK; not read]

**Charles Dickens as shorthand reporter**
- *David Copperfield* ch. 38 ("A Dissolution of Partnership") on learning Gurney's system: "a procession of new horrors, called arbitrary characters; the most despotic characters I have ever known; who insisted, for instance, that a thing like the beginning of a cobweb, meant expectation, and that a pen-and-ink sky-rocket, stood for disadvantageous." — [Project Gutenberg #766](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/766/pg766.txt) [PRIMARY, fiction]
- Ch. 43 ("Another Retrospect"): "I have tamed that savage stenographic mystery. … [I] am joined with eleven others in reporting the debates in Parliament for a Morning Newspaper. Night after night, I record predictions that never come to pass, professions that are never fulfilled, explanations that are only meant to mystify. I wallow in words." — [Project Gutenberg #766](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/766/pg766.txt) [PRIMARY, fiction]
- Speech to the Newspaper Press Fund, 20 May 1865: "I have often transcribed for the printer, from my shorthand notes, important public speeches in which the strictest accuracy was required, and a mistake in which would have been to a young man severely compromising, writing on the palm of my hand, by the light of a dark lantern, in a post-chaise and four, galloping through a wild country, and through the dead of the night, at the then surprising rate of fifteen miles an hour." — [Dickens, *Speeches: Literary and Social*, Project Gutenberg #824](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/824/pg824.txt) [PRIMARY]

### Inferences
- Gurney (1754) states the "clean verbatim" philosophy almost word for word, 250 years before modern style guides. He kept the stronger repeated answer and dropped the "imperfect" one. A modern ASR system does the opposite and keeps everything, including the imperfect answer.
- The Wilkes complaint shows the core weakness of a reporter-controlled record without review ("without any control or revision"). The judge's notes acted as an independent check, as the parties' stenographers did at Carthage.
- Johnson shows the far end of "sense over words": plausible speeches invented from names and positions. It is a historical warning about fluent but invented output, the same risk ASR "hallucinations" carry.

### Gaps
- **Timothy Bright, *Characterie* (1588):** the full title is usually given as *Characterie. An Arte of Shorte, Swifte, and Secrete Writing by Character*. I did not open a primary scan this session; verify against EEBO or Wikipedia's facsimile.
- **Isaac Pitman, 1837:** *Stenographic Sound-Hand* (1837) is the standard date. Not verified in this session; check the ODNB or a library catalogue.
- **"Whig dogs":** the remark ("I took care that the Whig dogs should not have the best of it") is widely attributed to Johnson, but it was **not found** in the abridged Boswell. Verify in the unabridged Boswell (Hill–Powell ed.) before quoting.
- **State Trials** (Cobbett/Howell, 1809–) and their editorial practice were not researched.
- Devereaux's articles ("The City and the Sessions Paper", *Journal of British Studies* 35 (1996); "The Fall of the Sessions Paper", *Criminal Justice History* 18 (2002)) are known only through Shoemaker's citations.

## Q5. Early Hansard and the 1907 "full report" definition

### Takeaway
The definition the brief quotes is real and widely reproduced. It appears in *Erskine May* (e.g., 25th ed., p. 151) as the meaning of the Official Report. Erskine May and the secondary literature trace it to the 1907 Select Committee on Parliamentary Debates. I verified the opening wording ("which, though not strictly verbatim, is substantially the verbatim report, with repetitions and redundancies omitted") via a parliamentary page. The closing clause ("leaves out nothing that adds to the meaning of the speech or illustrates the argument") is verified via Erskine May as quoted elsewhere. The middle clause "with obvious mistakes corrected" was **not** seen in any fetched text. In the 1908 Commons debate that put it into practice, the minister described it as "a verbatim report, trimmed of all those excrescences and redundancies".

### Cited Findings
- Cayman Islands Parliament "About Hansard" page, citing *Erskine May's Parliamentary Practice*, 25th ed., p. 151: the Official Report is "a full report, in the first person, of all speakers alike," a full report being one "which, though not strictly verbatim, is substantially the verbatim report, with repetitions and redundancies omitted" … — [Parliament of the Cayman Islands, About Hansard](https://parliament.ky/hansard/about-hansard/) [OFFICIAL, secondary Commonwealth source; the fetch tool truncated the rest of the sentence]
- Erskine May says the Official Report "leaves out nothing that adds to the meaning of the speech or illustrates the argument"; the wording is credited to the 1907 Select Committee on Parliamentary Debates. — [Wikipedia Talk:Hansard (pointer)](https://www.wikipedia.org/wiki/Talk:Hansard) [ENCYCLOPEDIA talk page; pointer only]
- Committee chronology: the Commons appointed a Select Committee on the cost and method of reporting debates (HC Deb 26 Mar 1907 vol 171 c1764) — [Hansard](https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/commons/1907/mar/26/parliamentary-debates); its report, minutes of evidence and appendix were presented on 4 July 1907 (HC Deb vol 177 c888) — [Hansard](https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/commons/1907/jul/04/parliamentary-debates) [PRIMARY/OFFICIAL]
- HC Deb 14 May 1908, vol 188, cols 1357–8. Charles Hobhouse (Financial Secretary to the Treasury), explaining "full" in its technical sense: "It means a verbatim report, trimmed of all those excrescences and redundancies with which Members are perhaps in the habit of filling up the matter of their speeches. In fact, a 'full' report puts into something like literary shape the efforts with which we endeavour to express our thoughts." Private Members' speeches had until then been reported "at not less than one-third of the total substance of what they say". — [Historic Hansard](https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/commons/1908/may/14/class-ii) [PRIMARY]
- Same debate, cols 1366–7. Arthur Lee: "it is a little difficult to understand what is meant by a full report. There is a long definition in the Report of the Committee … He talked about redundancies. … Then the hon. Gentleman spoke of putting speeches into literary shape. That is rather a large order." Hobhouse replied that he was referring to Members who, "when the proofs are delivered to them, [set] to work to trim them up", and Lee said he was "delighted to hear that it is proposed to stop that practice." — [Historic Hansard](https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/commons/1908/may/14/class-ii) [PRIMARY]. This is a direct contemporary debate about how much editing a "verbatim" record may carry, including speakers revising their own proofs, the same problem as Cicero's *Pro Milone*.
- Hansard's actual practice can go "well beyond the 'substantially verbatim'". A linguist reports "a sizeable gulf" between the stated aim and the editing. — [University of Westminster linguistics blog (via search summary)](https://blog.westminster.ac.uk/linguistics-events/wp-json/wp/v2/posts/291) [BLOG; academic author. The fuller peer-reviewed version is Mollin 2007, *Corpora* 2(2), "The Hansard hazard", not consulted here]
- US law uses the same idea: under the Jencks Act, "substantially verbatim" does not mean "precisely verbatim" (*Williams v. United States*, 338 F.2d 286, D.C. Cir. 1964). — [OpenJurist](https://m.openjurist.org/338/f2d/286) [PRIMARY case law, via search summary]
- Official shorthand writers: William Brodie Gurney (1777–1855) was formally appointed Official Shorthand Writer to both Houses by resolution in 1813. A Commons debate of 1840 refers to his holding the double appointment "ever since the year 1803". The post passed within the family (Joseph Gurney 1849; W. H. Gurney Salter 1872). — [Wikipedia, W. B. Gurney](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Brodie_Gurney); [HC Deb 5 Feb 1840, "Short-hand writers"](https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/commons/1840/feb/05/short-hand-writers) [ENCYCLOPEDIA + PRIMARY]. The Gurney family thus spans Old Bailey trial reporting (Thomas, 1748) to Parliament (into the 1870s).

### Inferences
- The 1907/Erskine May formula is a precise statement of the "sense with discipline" position. It allows deletion of repetition and correction of obvious slips, but forbids removing anything that "adds to the meaning". A court reporter can set this against the stricter courtroom norm, under which repetition and false starts may themselves carry meaning (e.g., witness hesitation).
- Lee's objection (1908) shows that legislators themselves saw "literary shape" as a threat to accuracy, so the "trimming" was disputed from the start.

### Gaps
- **Exact full wording of the 1907 definition, including "with obvious mistakes corrected", and the report's HC paper number** were not verified in a fetched primary text. Check the 1907 Report from the Select Committee on Parliamentary Debates (HMSO, 1907; a copy is catalogued at Royal Museums Greenwich as rmgl 44496) or a current Erskine May (erskinemay.parliament.uk returned HTTP 403 to my fetch).
- Thomas Hansard / William Cobbett origins (Cobbett's *Parliamentary Debates* from 1803/1804; Hansard's name from 1829) were not verified from a UK Parliament page in this session.

## Q6. Which 4–6 sources are strongest and most quotable for an academic paper?

### Takeaway
For a graduate paper, these six combine authority, citability and quotes that speak directly to the words-vs-sense question.

### Cited Findings
1. **Thucydides 1.22.1** (Crawley trans.; or Loeb, C. F. Smith). The founding statement of "general sense" over *akribeia* of words. — [Gutenberg Crawley](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/7142/pg7142.txt); [Greek, PerseusDL](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/PerseusDL/canonical-greekLit/master/data/tlg0003/tlg001/tlg0003.tlg001.perseus-grc2.xml)
2. **Asconius on *Pro Milone* + Cassius Dio 40.54.** Two versions of one speech, the stenographic (*excepta*) and the authored, with Milo's mullet joke. — [Asconius](https://www.thelatinlibrary.com/asconius.html); [Dio, Loeb/LacusCurtius](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Cassius_Dio/40*.html)
3. **Plutarch, *Cato Minor* 23.3.** The founding story of Roman shorthand in the Senate (Loeb, Perrin 1919). — [LacusCurtius](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Plutarch/Lives/Cato_Minor*.html)
4. **Conference of Carthage 411**, via Weidmann's CSEL 104 edition and Riedlberger's review. Rival stenographers reconcile notes and speakers approve, with a speaker's false starts still in the text. — [Plekos 2020](https://www.plekos.uni-muenchen.de/2020/r-weidmann.pdf)
5. **Robert Shoemaker, *Journal of British Studies* 47 (2008).** The best peer-reviewed treatment of trial-transcript fidelity, with the Gurney 1754 quote, Wilkes 1775, the 1778 "true, fair, and perfect narrative", and Langbein's verdict. — [White Rose PDF](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/4697/1/shoemakerr2.pdf)
6. **HC Deb 14 May 1908 + Erskine May's "full report" definition (1907 Select Committee).** The modern official compromise ("substantially the verbatim report") and the floor debate over "literary shape". — [Historic Hansard](https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/commons/1908/may/14/class-ii)
- Good epigraphs: Martial 14.208 (hand faster than tongue), Seneca Ep. 90.25 (shorthand as slaves' invention), Heywood ("scarce one word true"), Dickens's 1865 speech ("the strictest accuracy was required").

### Inferences
- Pairs that make the argument: Thucydides vs. Carthage (sense vs. verified verbatim), and Gurney 1754 vs. the 1778 City order (the reporter's judgement vs. the institution's demand for completeness).

### Gaps
- Books the paper would ideally cite but that were not read in this pass: Teitler, *Notarii and Exceptores* (1985); Boge, *Griechische Tachygraphie und Tironische Noten* (1973); Milne, *Greek Shorthand Manuals* (1934); Hoover (1953) on Johnson; Henderson in Mendle (ed.) 2001; Boyle 1981 on Montaillou; Mollin 2007 on Hansard. A library visit is needed for page-cited quotes from these.
