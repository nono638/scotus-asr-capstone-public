# What counts as the "correct" transcript: verbatim vs clean styles in the transcription industry and ASR research, and normalisation as editing

Source-type tags used throughout: **[PR]** peer-reviewed (journal or conference proceedings), **[PP]** preprint (arXiv, not peer-reviewed as far as the page shows), **[DOC]** company documentation or style guide, **[CODE]** code repository, **[BLOG]** company blog or marketing, **[2nd]** secondary or third-party summary. "UNVERIFIED" marks anything I could not confirm against the primary text.

Research date: 3 Nov 2026. The PDFs were read in full where noted ("read full text"). Otherwise only the abstract page or a search snippet was seen.

## 1. Industry definitions: verbatim, clean verbatim, intelligent verbatim, edited (Rev, Verbit, 3Play, GoTranscript, TranscribeMe), plus the Earnings-21/22 guidelines

### Takeaway
The industry has no single standard. Every vendor separates (a) verbatim, which keeps fillers, stutters, repetitions and false starts, from (b) a "clean", "non-verbatim" or "intelligent" style that removes them to make the text easier to read. Some vendors add (c) an "edited" tier that also fixes grammar. Rev's own style guide, the one used to make Earnings-21/22, is the most precise. Its default is non-verbatim, and that default also turns "gonna" into "going to". Earnings-21 and Earnings-22 deliberately ordered the **verbatim** product from Rev.

### Cited Findings

**Rev (the source of the Earnings-21/22 references)**
- Rev Transcription Style Guide (the file is titled "v4.0.1" in its URL, but the document header reads "v4.0.2") **[DOC]**, read full text. Its glossary: "Verbatim … This type of transcript requires the transcriptionist to include filler words, stutters and repetitions. Non-verbatim (default) … This type of transcript is the most common. The transcriptionist should lightly edit for readability." — [Rev Transcription Style Guide](https://cf-public.rev.com/styleguide/transcription/Rev+Transcription+Style+Guide+v4.0.1.pdf)
- Same guide, p.12: "Verbatim: In verbatim projects, transcribe exactly what you hear, including filler words, stutters, interjections (active listening) and repetitions." Also: "Default style (non-verbatim): In default style (non-verbatim) projects you should lightly edit for readability. You should not change the structure or meaning of the speech." — [Rev Style Guide](https://cf-public.rev.com/styleguide/transcription/Rev+Transcription+Style+Guide+v4.0.1.pdf)
- Same guide, the table of features that differ between the two styles. The verbatim/non-verbatim tick marks were lost in PDF extraction, so which column each row falls in is read from the worked examples. Rows: "Interjections or signs of active listening (e.g. Okay, Yeah, Mm-hmm (affirmative)) interjections that interrupt a speaker. Exception for non-verbatim: A direct response to a question…"; "Filler words (ums, uhs, and you know, like, etc.)"; "False starts / self-corrections that are quickly reworded, unless they provide additional context. A complete sentence is not a false start."; "Stutters"; "Intentional words spoken (e.g. probably, just, that, really, I think, well) even if the sentence 'sounds fine' without"; "Explicit content or profanity"; "Changing informal contractions to formal speech (e.g. gonna → going to, wanna → want to)". — [Rev Style Guide](https://cf-public.rev.com/styleguide/transcription/Rev+Transcription+Style+Guide+v4.0.1.pdf)
- Rev's worked example, verbatim: "And so, um, I guess... I think we should go to the, the m- m- movies tonight `cause of the discount (laughs)." Non-verbatim: "I think we should go to the movies tonight because of the discount." Second pair, verbatim: "I like, you know, called her, like, yesterday and, um, like, she was, like, sleeping." Non-verbatim: "I called her yesterday and she was sleeping." — [Rev Style Guide](https://cf-public.rev.com/styleguide/transcription/Rev+Transcription+Style+Guide+v4.0.1.pdf)
- Grammar rule, which applies to both styles: "Always transcribe exactly what is said. Although spoken word is not always grammatically correct, your transcript must preserve the integrity of the original speech. Do not write what you think the speaker meant to say." And: "Never correct or edit a speaker's grammar. a. Never change spoken contractions … b. Never change formal speech into informal contractions (e.g. going to → gonna, … you all → y'all) c. EXCEPTION: Always change informal contractions* to formal speech for Non-Verbatim (default) projects. (gonna → going to, wanna → want to)". The footnoted list of informal contractions: "'cause → because // 'em → them // doin' → doing // gonna → going to // gotta → got to …". So Rev's clean style edits dialectal and informal forms, even though it says it never corrects grammar. — [Rev Style Guide](https://cf-public.rev.com/styleguide/transcription/Rev+Transcription+Style+Guide+v4.0.1.pdf)
- The guide also tells transcribers to correct mispronunciations: "EXCEPTION: Correct egregious phonetic and pronunciation…" The worked example renders "refrigurator" as "refrigerator". — [Rev Style Guide](https://cf-public.rev.com/styleguide/transcription/Rev+Transcription+Style+Guide+v4.0.1.pdf)
- Rev blog definition, seen only as a search snippet, not fetched (the old URL returns 404): verbatim means capturing "every single word from an audio file in text, exactly the same way those words were originally spoken". Non-verbatim is "cleaned up to remove filler words, stammers, and anything that takes away from the core message". UNVERIFIED wording. — [Rev blog: What is verbatim transcription](https://www.rev.com/blog/resources/verbatim-transcription) **[BLOG]**

**Earnings-21 / Earnings-22 (both made by Rev)**
- Earnings-21, Del Rio et al. 2021, Interspeech 2021 **[PR]**, read full text: "To get accurate transcriptions, we used the Rev.com human transcription service. These audios were rigorously transcribed by a pool of experienced transcriptionists, then graded and reviewed by a different pool of senior transcriptionists… We chose to get 'verbatim' transcriptions that capture all speech utterances in exactly the same way those words were spoken - including filler words, false starts, grammatical errors, and other verbal cues or disfluencies… We found that verbatim transcripts are more useful for ASR evaluation." — [Del Rio et al. 2021, arXiv:2104.11348](https://arxiv.org/abs/2104.11348)
- Earnings-22, Del Rio, Ha, McNamara, Miller & Chandra 2022, submitted to Interspeech 2022 **[PP/PR]**, read full text. The corpus is 125 files, 119 hours of earnings calls. "To ensure high quality transcripts, we submitted our files to our own human transcription platform. Once completed, the quality of each transcript is also verified by a separate group of graders. Following our work in [3], we chose to produce verbatim transcriptions to best model real human speech. We processed the transcripts produced by Rev.com and removed atmospheric information [background music, coughs, other non-speech noises]." For its filled-pause and fragment analyses, the paper cites "Rev.com's style guide rules that provide fixed structure to key transcript features", linking Rev Transcription Style Guide v4.0.1. — [Del Rio et al. 2022, arXiv:2203.15591](https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.15591)
- Earnings-22 defines word fragments as "all tokens that ended in a '-'", which it says is possible because of "the 'verbatim' aspect of our transcripts". — [Del Rio et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.15591)

**Reverb: Rev's style-controllable ASR**
- Bhandari et al. 2024, Reverb **[PP]**: verbatim transcripts "include all speech sounds in the audio (including false starts, filler words, and laughter)". Non-verbatim transcripts are "lightly edited for readability". Rev's training corpus has 120,000 h of verbatim labels and 80,000 h of non-verbatim labels. "Training on both of these transcription styles is what enables the style control feature." A "verbatimicity parameter … anywhere between zero and one". "Halfway between verbatim and non-verbatim produces a reader-preferred style for captioning." — [Bhandari et al. 2024, arXiv:2410.03930](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.03930)

**Verbit (court-reporting vendor)**
- Verbit Editorial, "How and When to Use Clean Verbatim Transcription", 24 Jul 2023 **[BLOG]**: "Verbatim transcripts record all audio components and spoken dialogue exactly as they originally occurred", including "filler words, pauses, stammers, repetition and more." Clean verbatim is "a type of verbatim transcription that still captures all speech but removes audio components that may impede readability"; it "does not summarize or synthesize information." There is "no universally-accepted set of clean verbatim transcription rules." On legal use: "Clean verbatim wouldn't be appropriate or even allowed for these official proceedings"; witness testimony should "only use full verbatim transcripts that capture nuances in a speaker's tone and mental state." — [Verbit blog](https://verbit.ai/general/how-and-when-to-use-clean-verbatim-transcription)

**GoTranscript**
- Daniel Chang, GoTranscript blog, 8 Sep 2022 **[BLOG]**, four tiers. Full (true) verbatim "Includes every word and sound, including filler words, repeated words, laughter, and background noises", described as "Best for legal or strict research purposes". Verbatim (standard) "Captures everything the speaker says but typically omits non-speech sounds". Intelligent verbatim (clean verbatim) "Edits out most fillers and repetitive sounds for cleaner reading". Edited transcription "Removes filler words (such as 'um,' 'uh,' and 'like')", "Smooths out stammers, repetitions, and false starts", and "Repairs obvious grammar mistakes and awkward phrasing". — [GoTranscript blog](https://gotranscript.com/blog/verbatim-transcription)

**3Play Media, TranscribeMe, and the naming problem**
- 3Play Media, captioning slide deck (search snippet only, UNVERIFIED wording) **[BLOG]**: "if your video is scripted content, then you'll want ensure your captions are by verbatim … For lectures or live captioning, a clean read is preferable, meaning you'll want to eliminate the filler words." I found no 3Play style guide with formal definitions. — [3Play slides](https://www.slideshare.net/3Play/quick-start-to-captioning-84173007)
- A James Madison University captioning guide (snippet) defines true verbatim as "transcribing everything as said, including stammers, repetitive speech, false starts, etc." and cites Verbit's definition of clean verbatim. **[2nd]** — [JMU guidelines](https://www.jmu.edu/accessibility/digital-accessibility/guides/captioning/transcription-guidelines.shtml)
- TranscribeMe keeps separate "Full Verbatim" and "Clean Verbatim" style guides. The help pages fetched contain only tables of contents: articles on "Filler and Crutch Words", "False Starts", and "Feedback and Lead-In Words". The full rules sit in a PDF ("CV-TranscribeMe-Style-Guide-v3.2-15Sep2022") I could not open. A search snippet from a TranscribeMe blog says "edited verbatim" goes further by improving grammar and tenses (UNVERIFIED). **[DOC]** — [TM Clean Verbatim Style Guide](https://tm-library.helpscoutdocs.com/article/469-tm-clean-verbatim-style-guide); [TM Full Verbatim Style Guide](https://tm-library.helpscoutdocs.com/article/557-team-guides-full-verbatim-style-guide)
- The labels do not agree across sources. Some treat "intelligent verbatim" as a synonym for clean verbatim (GoTranscript). Others treat it as a more subjective tier where the transcriber decides what to omit (search-result summaries of alphabetsecretarial.co.uk and koji.so, **[2nd]**, UNVERIFIED). — [GoTranscript](https://gotranscript.com/blog/verbatim-transcription)

### Inferences
- Across vendors, the "clean" styles share one core: delete fillers (um/uh), stutters, quick false starts and repetitions, and often discourse fillers ("you know", "like"). They disagree on grammar correction (no for Verbit and Rev; yes for GoTranscript's "edited" style) and on informal contractions. Rev's default style turns "gonna", "'cause" and "'em" into standard forms. That is a dialect-levelling edit in a style the vendor describes as not changing grammar.
- For the capstone: Earnings-22's official references are Rev **verbatim**. A model that writes in a clean style, such as Whisper, is therefore penalised on Earnings-22 for every filler and stutter it drops, and for every "gonna" it writes as "going to" (unless scoring normalises these away). McNamara et al. (Section 3) measure exactly this effect.
- Strongest to cite: the Rev Style Guide (primary document behind the Earnings-22 references); Del Rio et al. 2021 (Interspeech, peer-reviewed) for the choice of verbatim; Bhandari et al. 2024 for Rev's own description of the two styles. Vendor blogs (Verbit, GoTranscript) are fine for definitions but should be labelled as industry sources.

### Gaps
- No formal 3Play Media definition found. TranscribeMe's full rules sit in a PDF I could not retrieve.
- I could not confirm from a Verbit page whether Verbit defines "intelligent verbatim" or "edited" as separate products.
- The Rev style guide fetched is v4.0.2, current at the time of fetching. Which version was in force when Earnings-22 (2022) was transcribed is not stated beyond the v4.0.1 link in the paper.

## 2. Research corpora's transcription conventions (LDC Switchboard/Fisher quick vs careful, CORAAL, EdAcc) and how WER depends on them

### Takeaway
The research corpora were also transcribed to written conventions that differ from one another. LDC's "quick transcription" (used for Fisher) treats a dropped disfluency as insignificant. CORAAL is explicitly "not … an exact, textually accurate representation": it records grammatical features of African American Language but standardises pronunciation. EdAcc was punctuation-stripped and spelling-normalised by its authors for scoring. Two transcribers following the same LDC guidelines disagree by about 4–10% WER depending on method and genre, so differences of that size are noise between the references themselves.

### Cited Findings

**LDC quick (QTR) vs careful (CTR) transcription**
- Glenn, Strassel, Lee, Maeda, Zakhary & Li (2010), "Transcription Methods for Consistency, Volume and Efficiency", LREC 2010 **[PR]**, read full text. QTR lets "a transcriber … complete one hour of data in approximately 5 hours for English". "The QTR approach has since been adopted for the creation of training corpora, such as the 2003 Fisher English corpus and NIST Rich Transcription evaluations." In QTR, transcribers "type what they hear, ignoring capitalization or punctuation rules, but marking a restricted set of non-lexemes." — [Glenn et al. 2010 (PDF)](http://www.lrec-conf.org/proceedings/lrec2010/pdf/849_Paper.pdf); [ACL Anthology L10-1587](https://aclanthology.org/L10-1587/)
- Same paper: "Technology evaluations often require gold-standard references, which are produced with a Careful Transcription (CTR) method that involves multiple quality control passes … Elements of a careful transcript include a verbatim transcript; time-alignment …" — [Glenn et al. 2010](http://www.lrec-conf.org/proceedings/lrec2010/pdf/849_Paper.pdf)
- Inter-transcriber "Word Disagreement Rate" (WDR, computed with sclite). The authors chose this term "Since not all of the transcription 'errors' are truly mistakes". English conversational telephone speech: careful 4.1–4.5%; quick 9.63% (5 pairs). English broadcast conversation: careful 1.3%, quick 3.5% (as I read the table layout). Other quick figures: meetings 6.23%, interviews 3.84%. "For CTS data [WDR] reached 4.1-4.5%; close examination of the discrepancies revealed that 95% were marked as 'judgment calls' due to contractions, rapid or difficult speech, or disfluencies (Strassel, 2004)." — [Glenn et al. 2010](http://www.lrec-conf.org/proceedings/lrec2010/pdf/849_Paper.pdf)
- Same paper, adjudication of English quick transcripts: about 15% of differences were real transcriber errors, 65% were "insignificant differences" (capitalisation, punctuation, spelling of hesitations or partial words), and nearly 20% were "judgment calls". Key line: "For quick transcription approaches, omitting a disfluency is considered insignificant, since the goal of Q(R)TR is to produce content words for every utterance." In a 2008 study of meeting data, "64% of all dually-transcribed segments demonstrated some amount of disagreement." — [Glenn et al. 2010](http://www.lrec-conf.org/proceedings/lrec2010/pdf/849_Paper.pdf)

**CORAAL**
- Kendall & Farrington (eds.), *CORAAL User Guide*, version 2018.10.06 **[DOC]**, read the transcription section. Three rounds: an undergraduate first pass, a graduate-student full edit, then a redaction pass. "A primary goal of a time-aligned transcript is to act as a proxy to the original recording … It is not to make an exact, textually accurate representation of the speech. Along these lines, orthographic transcription conventions use simple orthography and standard-like spelling. As a general rule, morphosyntactic variants (e.g. was for were) are transcribed, but phonological variants (such as velar nasal fronting (in for ing), r-lessness, and dialectal vowel qualities) are not." — [CORAAL User Guide (2018.10.06)](https://talkbank.org/ca/access/0docs/CORAAL.pdf)
- Same guide: transcripts "accurately account for all the 'noises' of speech, such as laughter, filled pauses (like 'uh' or 'um'), and restarts (e.g 'I- I- I di- didn't mean to') as well as misspoken words." On filled pauses: "Only uh, um, and clear cases of ah are used." Numbers are written as words ("twenty-two years"). — [CORAAL User Guide](https://talkbank.org/ca/access/0docs/CORAAL.pdf)
- A fixed list of reduced forms: musta, woulda, shoulda, gonna ~ I'm'a, hafta, tryna, sposta, finna, gotta, wanna, oughta, cause, til, 'em, lemme, whatchu/whatcha, gotcha. "Some commonly reduced constructions (e.g. used to, kind of, sort of, out of) are never orthographically represented as reduced." Quotative "talmbout" is "transcribed as talking about". — [CORAAL User Guide](https://talkbank.org/ca/access/0docs/CORAAL.pdf)
- AAL morphosyntax is kept: "for clearly morphological/syntactic features, transcribers were instructed to transcribe as close to the audio as possible." Examples: "So she run over to her bag" (3sg -s absence), "We was over my uh, father mother house" (possessive -s absence), "friends with they kids" (possessive they). Where it is unclear whether a feature is phonological or grammatical, transcribers "err on the side of using standard orthographical conventions (i.e. transcribed the d in 'The boy named Bill')". Dialect lexicon kept: aight, aks, finna, brazy, hisself, etc. — [CORAAL User Guide](https://talkbank.org/ca/access/0docs/CORAAL.pdf)

**EdAcc**
- Sanabria, Bogoychev, Markl, Carmantini, Klejch & Bell (2023), "The Edinburgh International Accents of English Corpus", arXiv:2303.18110 (ICASSP 2023 per the authors' listing; venue UNVERIFIED from the PDF) **[PP/PR]**, read full text. About 40 h of dyadic video calls. "All conversations were manually transcribed by multiple professional transcribers. Each turn was manually segmented and orthographically transcribed, including overlaps between speakers, noise, laughter and hesitations." Then: "We made them consistent with orthography, and lexicon used in public systems - we removed punctuation, and normalized ambiguous spelling. We localized sounds (eg., laughs) and words that a system can transcribe in multiple manners (eg., numbers) and ignore them during scoring." Footnote: "the public release of the dataset does not contain any normalization." Best model (Whisper) reached 19.7% WER on the test set, against 2.7% on LibriSpeech test-clean. — [Sanabria et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.18110)

### Inferences
- The LDC result that quick transcripts disagree about twice as much as careful ones (9.6% vs 4.1–4.5% on English CTS) is the closest published analogue to the capstone's 13.4% gap between two Earnings-22 references. The capstone gap is larger, which fits a deliberate style difference (verbatim vs clean) on top of ordinary transcriber noise. That is an inference; the corpora and genres differ.
- CORAAL's own conventions already "standardise" pronunciation but not grammar. So when an ASR system writes "were" for a speaker's "was", or "their" for "they", WER against CORAAL counts it as an error. An ASR-side grammar standardisation is visible in CORAAL scoring, while pronunciation standardisation is not.
- EdAcc's released references are un-normalised, and its authors' scoring ignored laughs and numbers. Anyone re-scoring EdAcc makes their own normalisation choices, so WER values across papers are not directly comparable.
- Strongest to cite: Glenn et al. 2010 (LREC, peer-reviewed, the primary source for QTR/CTR agreement numbers); the CORAAL User Guide (primary documentation; cite the version used); Sanabria et al. 2023.

### Gaps
- I read CORAAL User Guide version 2018.10.06. Later CORAAL releases (2021+) may have revised conventions. Check the guide that ships with the version used in the capstone.
- I did not find EdAcc's transcriber instructions themselves (verbatim or clean). The paper says only "orthographically transcribed, including … hesitations".
- The Fisher corpus paper (Cieri et al. 2004) and the LDC QTR guideline document were not fetched directly. QTR details come from Glenn et al. 2010.

## 3. Human-vs-human disagreement in ASR research (human parity, LDC agreement, two references for Earnings-22)

### Takeaway
"Human WER" is itself an artefact of transcription method. Microsoft measured 5.9% (Switchboard) and 11.3% (CallHome) using an ordinary two-pass vendor pipeline. IBM measured 5.1% and 6.8% using three transcribers, a fourth senior checker, and LDC guidelines. Older estimates (Lippmann's ~4%) had weak sourcing. The most directly relevant study is Rev's own (McNamara et al. 2024). It made verbatim and non-verbatim references for a 10-hour Earnings-22 subset and showed that model WER swings by up to a factor of 2 depending on the reference: the OpenAI (Whisper-derived) API scored 13.11% against verbatim but 6.41% against non-verbatim.

### Cited Findings

**Microsoft human parity**
- Xiong, Droppo, Huang, Seide, Seltzer, Stolcke, Yu & Zweig, "Achieving Human Parity in Conversational Speech Recognition", MSR-TR-2016-71, arXiv:1610.05256 v2 (Feb 2017) **[PP]**, read full text. "The error rate of professional transcribers is 5.9% for the Switchboard portion … and 11.3% for the CallHome portion". The system scored 5.8% and 11.0%. — [Xiong et al. 2016](https://arxiv.org/abs/1610.05256)
- Method: "we leveraged an existing pipeline in which Microsoft data is transcribed on a weekly basis. This pipeline uses a large commercial vendor to perform two-pass transcription. In the first pass, a transcriber works from scratch … In the second pass, a second listener monitors the data to do error correction… The intention was to measure the error rate of professional transcribers going about their normal everyday business… we did not do a complex multi-party transcription and adjudication process." The figures "should be taken as an indication of the 'error rate' of a trained professional working in industry-standard speech transcript production." — [Xiong et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1610.05256)
- On earlier figures: "human performance on this task has been widely cited as being 4% … However, the error rate estimate … is attributed to a 'personal communication,' and the actual source of this number is ephemeral." — [Xiong et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1610.05256)
- On training-data noise: "the bulk of the Fisher training data … was transcribed with the 'quick transcription' guidelines. Thus, the current state of the art is actually far exceeding the noise level in its own training data." — [Xiong et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1610.05256)
- Error analysis: humans and machine err alike, except the machine confuses hesitation "uh" with backchannel "uh-huh". The authors trace this to Fisher's quick transcription: "there is inconsistent treatment of backchannel and hesitation in the resulting data." Humans had "a somewhat lower substitution rate, and a higher deletion rate", which "might reflect a human bias to avoid outputting uncertain information, or the productivity demands on a professional transcriber." The "single most common error made by the transcribers was to omit the word 'I.'" — [Xiong et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1610.05256)
- Caution: the paper's conclusion paragraph says the human error rates were "5.8% and 11.0%", which are the machine figures. This looks like a slip in the paper; cite the abstract numbers (5.9/11.3). The expanded journal version (IEEE/ACM TASLP 2017, "Toward human parity…") was not checked: UNVERIFIED.

**IBM rebuttal**
- Saon, Kurata, Sercu, Audhkhasi, Thomas, Dimitriadis, Cui, Ramabhadran, Picheny, Lim, Roomi & Hall (2017), "English Conversational Telephone Speech Recognition by Humans and Machines", arXiv:1703.02136 (published at Interspeech 2017; venue UNVERIFIED from the arXiv page) **[PP/PR]**, read full text. Transcription was done by Appen: "three independent transcribers provide transcripts which were quality checked by a fourth senior transcriber… The transcribers were familiarized with the LDC transcription guidelines which cover hyphenations, spelled abbreviations, contractions, partial words, nonspeech sounds, etc." The first pass took 12–14× real time, plus 1.7–2× for QC. Before scoring, non-speech markers and partial words were made non-lexical and punctuation was removed. — [Saon et al. 2017](https://arxiv.org/abs/1703.02136)
- Results (Table 1). SWB: raw 6.1 / 5.3 / 5.7, after QC 5.6 / 5.1 / 5.2. CH: raw 8.7 / 6.9 / 8.0, after QC 7.8 / 6.8 / 7.6. "we were expecting the SWB measurement to be closer to the Lippmann estimate of 4% but could only get down to 5.1% for the best transcriber after quality checks." The CallHome gap (6.8% vs Microsoft's 11.3%) "can be attributed to a much lower deletion rate for our best human transcript." Conclusion: "the human WER of expert transcribers that were asked to do a high-quality job is simply lower than what was previously reported." — [Saon et al. 2017](https://arxiv.org/abs/1703.02136)

**Stolcke & Droppo 2017**
- "Comparing Human and Machine Errors in Conversational Speech Transcription", Proc. Interspeech 2017, pp. 137–141 **[PR]**, abstract only. The commonest human and machine errors overlap heavily. The exception is that the recognizer confuses filled pauses ("uh") with backchannels ("uhhuh"), which humans rarely do. The authors note "some debate how to precisely quantify human performance". — [Stolcke & Droppo 2017, arXiv:1708.08615](https://arxiv.org/abs/1708.08615)

**Lippmann 1997**
- R. P. Lippmann, "Speech recognition by machines and humans", *Speech Communication* 22(1):1–15, 1997 **[PR]**. Seen only as cited by Saon et al. and Xiong et al. for the ~4% human estimate; I did not read the paper itself. UNVERIFIED beyond the citation details. — cited in [Saon et al. 2017](https://arxiv.org/abs/1703.02136)

**Two references for Earnings-22 (the closest study to the capstone)**
- McNamara, del Río Fernández, Bhandari, Ratajczak, Chen, Miller & Jetté (10 Dec 2024), "Style-agnostic evaluation of ASR using multiple reference transcripts", arXiv:2412.07937 **[PP]**, all Rev authors, read via the HTML full text. Each recording has a verbatim (V) and a non-verbatim (NV) Rev human transcript. V is "exactly what you hear, including filler words, stutters, interjections (active listening) and repetitions"; NV is "lightly edit[ed] for readability". The two are aligned and merged into a multi-reference FST. "GOLD" WER scores only regions where V and NV agree. Data: Rev16 (podcasts) and "Earnings-22 Sub 10", a random 10 h subset. Conclusion: "existing WER reports are likely significantly over-estimating the number of contentful errors." — [McNamara et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.07937)
- Earnings-22 Sub 10 results (Table 2b, columns V-WER / NV-WER / multi-ref / GOLD): Parakeet-TDT-1.1B 14.94 / 12.16 / 9.76 / 8.85; Parakeet-CTC-1.1B 14.70 / 12.22 / 9.62 / 8.63; OpenAI API 13.11 / 6.41 / 4.50 / 3.83 (the authors assume a Whisper large-v3 derivative; Whisper itself was not benchmarked separately); RevAI V 7.74 / 14.57 / 6.35 / 5.86; RevAI NV 14.06 / 5.37 / 5.29 / 4.62; Canary-1B 14.83 / 8.26 / 15.69 / 15.02 (inflated by one hallucinated file). Rev's V model scores best against V references and its NV model against NV references. The authors acknowledge a possible bias in Rev's favour because Rev made the references. The paper does **not** report the WER between the V and NV references themselves. — [McNamara et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.07937)

**Reverb style results**
- Bhandari et al. 2024, Rev16 (Table 2), WER against verbatim / non-verbatim references: Reverb 7.99 / 7.06; Whisper large-v3 10.67 / 11.37; Canary-1B 13.82 / 13.24. Earnings-21/22 results in that paper are verbatim-only. — [Bhandari et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.03930)

**Whisper vs human transcription services**
- Radford et al. 2023 (Whisper), ICML 2023 **[PR]**, read full text. On 25 Kincaid46 recordings, compared with 4 human transcription services and 1 computer-assisted service: "the computer-assisted service has the lowest aggregate WER that is 1.15% point better than Whisper's, and the pure-human performance is only a fraction of a percentage point better than Whisper's." Scored after Whisper's own normaliser. — [Radford et al. 2023, arXiv:2212.04356](https://arxiv.org/abs/2212.04356)

**Szymański et al. 2020**
- "WER we are and WER we think we are", Findings of EMNLP 2020 (the arXiv page says "Accepted to EMNLP Findings") **[PR]**. Three commercial ASR systems tested on real conversations and HUB'05 gave WERs "significantly higher than the best reported results". The paper calls for new datasets "with high quality annotations". It is about benchmark realism more than transcription style. It says spontaneous conversations are "riddled with various disfluencies (discourse markers, filled pauses, back-channeling)". — [Szymański et al. 2020, arXiv:2010.03432](https://arxiv.org/abs/2010.03432)

### Inferences
- The capstone's 13.4% disagreement between two human references for the same Earnings-22 calls is larger than Microsoft's measured human WER on Switchboard (5.9%), IBM's (5.1%), and LDC's careful (4.1–4.5%) and quick (9.6%) inter-transcriber rates. That is consistent with the two references following different style rules rather than with ordinary mishearing. I found no published V-vs-NV reference WER to compare it with directly; McNamara et al. did not report one.
- McNamara's Table 2b supports the capstone's "Whisper writes clean, Parakeet writes verbatim-ish" finding only in part. The Whisper-derived OpenAI API halves its WER when the reference switches from verbatim to non-verbatim (13.11 → 6.41), a strong clean-style signature. Parakeet-TDT moves much less (14.94 → 12.16). That points to Parakeet sitting between the two styles, not fully verbatim. Note the opposite direction on Rev16 in the Reverb paper, where Whisper large-v3 is slightly *better* against verbatim (10.67 vs 11.37). Scoring normalisation, such as removing fillers before scoring, probably explains such reversals, but the papers do not state their normalisation fully. Flag this as unresolved.
- Strongest to cite: Xiong et al. (MSR tech report / arXiv; the TASLP version is preferable if verified) together with Saon et al. (Interspeech 2017) as a pair showing human WER depends on method; Glenn et al. 2010 for LDC agreement; McNamara et al. 2024 (preprint, but the only direct Earnings-22 two-reference study; note the Rev authorship).

### Gaps
- No published WER between the V and NV Earnings-22 references (McNamara et al. do not give one). The capstone's 13.4% may be new.
- I did not find an "LDC inter-transcriber agreement" study beyond Glenn et al. 2010 and the Strassel (2004) EARS work it cites. Strassel 2004 itself was not located.
- McNamara et al.'s exact text normalisation before WER was not checked in detail.

## 4. Normalisation as an editorial choice (Whisper normaliser, ESB, Open ASR Leaderboard, sclite/GLM, fstalign, disfluency removal, Careless Whisper)

### Takeaway
Every major benchmark edits text before scoring, and those edits mostly push references toward the "clean" style. The Whisper normaliser, reused by the Open ASR Leaderboard, deletes um/uh/hmm/mm/mhm/mmm and expands "gonna" and "wanna". OpenAI built it "through iterative manual inspection" of where WER penalised Whisper, and warned it may be overfitted to Whisper's style. ESB argued the opposite: keep orthography and score punctuation and case. The Open ASR Leaderboard paper itself suggests separating tasks "based on whether … verbatim transcription is required". A separate research line treats disfluency removal as a target output ("readable" or "intended" transcripts).

### Cited Findings

**Whisper's normaliser**
- Radford et al. 2023 §3.2 **[PR]**: "WER … penalizes all differences between the model's output and the reference transcript including innocuous differences in transcript style… it is particularly acute for zero-shot models like Whisper, which do not observe any examples of specific datasets transcript formats… We opt to address this problem with extensive standardization of text before the WER calculation to minimize penalization of non-semantic differences. Our text normalizer was developed through iterative manual inspection to identify common patterns where naive WER penalized Whisper models for an innocuous difference." "For several datasets, we observe WER drops of up to 50 percent usually due to a quirk such as a dataset's reference transcripts seperating contractions from words with whitespace. We caution this development procedure comes at a risk of overfitting to the transcription style of Whisper models." — [Radford et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2212.04356)
- §4.4: "there is a risk that our normalizer is overfitted to fixing Whisper's peculiarities rather than addressing general variation in transcription." Compared with the FairSpeech normaliser (Koenecke et al. 2020), it reduced Whisper's WER "significantly more" on WSJ, CallHome and Switchboard, e.g. by not penalising "you're" vs "you are". — [Radford et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2212.04356)
- Appendix C steps include: "Remove any phrases between matching brackets"; "Remove any phrases between matching parentheses"; "Remove any of the following words: hmm, mm, mhm, mmm, uh, um"; "Convert standard or informal contracted forms of English into the original form"; British-to-American spelling; spelled-out numbers to digits. — [Radford et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2212.04356)
- Code **[CODE]**: ignore pattern `r"\b(hmm|mm|mhm|mmm|uh|um)\b"`; replacers include `wanna → want to`, `gotta → got to`, `gonna → going to`, `won't → will not`, `ain't → aint`, `n't → not`, `'re → are`, `'ll → will`, `'ve → have`, `'m → am`, `mr → mister`. — [openai/whisper normalizers/english.py](https://github.com/openai/whisper/blob/main/whisper/normalizers/english.py)

**ESB: the case for orthographic scoring**
- Gandhi, von Platen & Rush (2022), "ESB: A Benchmark For Multi-Domain End-to-End Speech Recognition", arXiv:2210.13352 (submitted to ICLR 2023) **[PP]**, read full text. "many speech recognition systems require dataset-specific tuning (audio filtering, punctuation removal and normalisation of casing), therefore assuming a-priori knowledge of both the audio and text distributions." "we retain all dataset-specific transcription requirements (punctuation and casing) in our evaluation … This decision leads to an orthographic WER." Its ablation applies "the full English text normaliser from Radford et al. (2022), which removes filler words ('uh', 'uhm', 'mhm'), standardises number formatting … and makes spellings consistent." ESB includes Earnings-22 (punctuated and cased). ESB's cleaning of junk tags (<unk>, <noise>) is "not … any form of normalisation". — [Gandhi et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.13352)

**Open ASR Leaderboard**
- Srivastav, Zheng, Bezzam, Le Bihan, Moumen, Gandhi et al. (Oct 2025, revised to v4 Mar 2026), arXiv:2510.06961 **[PP]**, read full text. "For normalized transcriptions, punctuation and casing is removed, as well as disfluencies such as fillers (e.g., 'ah', 'uh', 'um'), repetitions, and repairs." "Not all models produce transcripts with punctuation, casing, or disfluencies; in particular, some models explicitly remove the latter… we normalize all text prior to computing WER. This normalization removes punctuation and casing, and applies an English text normalization pipeline closely following that of Whisper." Conclusion: "because models vary in how they handle disfluencies, it may be valuable to differentiate tasks based on whether disfluencies are explicitly modeled or whether verbatim transcription is required." The datasets table lists Earnings-22 among sets whose transcripts are "Punctuated, cased, disfluencies". — [Srivastav et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.06961)

**NIST sclite / GLM**
- SCTK sclite documentation **[DOC]** (from the search summary, not read in full): "SCLITE includes the ability to treat marked reference and hypothesis words as optional… such optional words are tallied as correct" (the "optionally deletable" mode, added in rev 1.4a, 1997). In standard Hub5 practice, hesitations are mapped to %HESITATION via a GLM file and made optionally deletable, and contractions are written as alternations (e.g. I'M => { I'M / I AM }). The exact flag behaviour should be checked against the manual: UNVERIFIED detail. — [SCTK sclite docs](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/usnistgov/SCTK/master/doc/sclite.htm); [LDC Hub5 eval scoring notes](https://catalog.ldc.upenn.edu/docs/LDC97S66/H496EVSC.TXT)
- Saon et al. 2017 confirms the practice for human transcripts: non-speech markers and partial words "were tagged as non-lexical items which are ignored during scoring". — [Saon et al. 2017](https://arxiv.org/abs/1703.02136)

**fstalign (Rev)**
- Earnings-21 paper §3.1 **[PR]**: "our tool uses a curated list of common word transforms that enable synonymous tokens to be leniently substituted … going to - gonna; I'll - I will … for the reference 'I'm going to win.', 'I'm gonna win.' would be a penalty-free hypothesis. In Earnings21, these synonym-transformations typically affect 0.3% of the potential transcript disagreements." Text-normalisation tags (e.g. YEAR: "twenty twenty one" / "two thousand twenty one") "affect roughly 5.0% of reference tokens." — [Del Rio et al. 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2104.11348)
- The fstalign README **[CODE]** covers case and punctuation scoring options (`--use-case`, `--use-punctuation`). The README page I fetched said nothing on fillers; filler handling would be in docs/Usage.md (not checked). — [revdotcom/fstalign](https://github.com/revdotcom/fstalign)

**Disfluency removal and "readable"/"intended" transcripts as the target**
- Liao et al. (2021), "Generating Human Readable Transcript for Automatic Speech Recognition with Pre-trained Language Model", ICASSP 2021 (Microsoft) **[PR]** (search summary only): ASR output is hard to read because of disfluencies and fillers even when recognition is accurate; they fine-tune RoBERTa on MDE-derived data to produce readable text, scored with a readability-aware WER and BLEU. — [Liao et al. 2021, arXiv:2102.11114](https://arxiv.org/abs/2102.11114)
- Jamshid Lou & Johnson, "End-to-End Speech Recognition and Disfluency Removal" (Findings of EMNLP 2020; venue UNVERIFIED) **[PP/PR]** (search summary only): a single model maps disfluent speech to fluent text, slightly worse than ASR plus a separate disfluency detector. — [arXiv:2009.10298](https://arxiv.org/abs/2009.10298)
- Wagner, Thallinger & Zusag (2024), "CrisperWhisper: Accurate Timestamps on Verbatim Speech Transcriptions", arXiv:2408.16589 (Interspeech 2024; venue UNVERIFIED) **[PP/PR]**, read full text: "Recent works [8, 9] show that Whisper eliminates many filler words, recurring utterances and other artifacts, which Lea et al. [10] refer to as an intended transcription style, suitable for contexts where clarity of intent is prioritized over detailed speech analysis. This style, however, does not support the detection, categorization, or analysis of disfluencies." They fine-tune Whisper toward verbatim output on AMI and PodcastFillers. — [Wagner et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.16589)
- Lea et al. (2023), "From user perceptions to technical improvement: Enabling people who stutter to better use speech recognition", CHI 2023, pp. 1–16 **[PR]**, cited by CrisperWhisper as the source of the "intended" vs verbatim transcription distinction. Not read directly: UNVERIFIED wording. — via [Wagner et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.16589)

**Careless Whisper**
- Koenecke, Choi, Mei, Schellmann & Sloane (2024), "Careless Whisper: Speech-to-Text Hallucination Harms", ACM FAccT '24 **[PR]**, read full text. "roughly 1% of audio transcriptions contained entire hallucinated phrases or sentences which did not exist in any form in the underlying audio"; "38% of hallucinations include explicit harms" (violence 19%, inaccurate associations 13%, false authority 8%). Speakers with aphasia had more transcriptions with hallucinations (1.7% vs 1.2% for controls; on a matched subset, 1.8% vs 1.1%). The authors link this to longer non-verbal pauses. — [Koenecke et al. 2024, arXiv:2402.08021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.08021)

### Inferences
- The Whisper/Open ASR normaliser erases exactly the features that separate Rev verbatim from non-verbatim (fillers; gonna → going to). Under it, much of the "clean vs verbatim" difference disappears from WER. Repetitions, stutters and false starts are not removed by the regex, so they still count. The capstone should state which normaliser it uses and report results both with and without filler removal. The Whisper code removes only six filler tokens; Open ASR's prose says "repetitions, and repairs" are removed from normalised *dataset* transcriptions, which is a dataset-prep step, not part of the regex.
- OpenAI's own words ("developed through iterative manual inspection to identify … where naive WER penalized Whisper", and the overfitting caveat) let the paper argue that normalisation is an editorial act tuned toward one system's style.
- "Readable", "intended" and "clean" transcription is an explicit research target (Liao et al.; Lea et al.). Whisper's clean style is therefore a design outcome, not just an error pattern. Hallucination (Careless Whisper) is a different kind of error, invention rather than tidying, and it matters for court use.
- Strongest to cite: Radford et al. 2023 (ICML) and the normaliser code; Gandhi et al. 2022 (ESB) for the orthographic counter-position; Srivastav et al. 2025 for current leaderboard practice and its own verbatim caveat; Koenecke et al. 2024 (FAccT, peer-reviewed); Wagner et al. 2024 for "Whisper eliminates many filler words".

### Gaps
- I found no paper titled "Spoken Language Formatting" from Microsoft; the closest Microsoft paper is Liao et al. 2021. Not verified further.
- The fstalign docs on filler handling and the Hub5 GLM file contents were not read directly.
- Whether Whisper's normaliser maps CORAAL forms such as "tryna", "finna" or "sposta": the replacer entries I saw include gonna, wanna and gotta. I did not check the full dictionary for tryna or finna. Check `english.py` before claiming either way.

## 5. ASR bias as a transcription-style issue (Koenecke 2020, Martin & Tang 2020, Wassink 2022, "standardising" dialect)

### Takeaway
The key bias papers measure ASR against dialect-faithful references (CORAAL), but they also normalise those references. Koenecke et al. themselves changed "aks" to "ask" and normalised "gonna" and "tryna". They attribute most of the gap to acoustic models. Martin & Tang show a grammatical case: habitual "be" is misrecognised more often, plausibly because ASR language models rewrite it into Standard English constructions ("I be" → "I'll be"). That is the "ASR standardises grammar" argument in its clearest peer-reviewed form.

### Cited Findings
- Koenecke, Nam, Lake, Nudell, Quartey, Mengesha, Toups, Rickford, Jurafsky & Goel (2020), "Racial disparities in automated speech recognition", *PNAS* 117(14):7684–7689, doi:10.1073/pnas.1915768117 **[PR]**, fetched via PMC. Five systems (Amazon, Apple, Google, IBM, Microsoft): average WER 0.35 for Black speakers vs 0.19 for white speakers (2,141 matched snippets each). Best was Microsoft, 0.27 vs 0.15; worst was Apple, 0.45 vs 0.23. — [Koenecke et al. 2020 (PNAS)](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.1915768117); [PMC full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7149386/)
- Same paper, reference cleaning (per the PMC text): nonstandard spellings changed, e.g. "aks" → "ask"; informal spellings such as "gonna" and "tryna" normalised to standard forms; fillers ("um", "uh") and expletives removed; flags for unintelligible or redacted speech removed; numbers converted to digits; text lowercased and punctuation removed. — [Koenecke et al. 2020 (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7149386/)
- Same paper, cause: disparities "arise primarily from a performance gap in the acoustic models". On 206 identical phrases, WER for Black speakers was about twice that for white speakers (e.g. Apple 0.28 vs 0.12). Copula-absence phrases had higher language-model perplexity than their Standard English versions. Caveat: the dialect-density measure mixed phonological and grammatical features. — [Koenecke et al. 2020 (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7149386/)
- Martin & Tang (2020), "Understanding Racial Disparities in Automatic Speech Recognition: The Case of Habitual 'be'", Proc. Interspeech 2020, pp. 626–630 **[PR]**, read full text. Over 100 h of CORAAL; DeepSpeech and Google Cloud Speech. "habitual 'be' and its surrounding words were more error prone than non-habitual 'be' and its surrounding words", controlling for context, noise and speech rate. Mechanism: "Because it is probable that the language model component of most ASRs have not been trained on AAE linguistic structures, utterances containing habitual 'be' may be incorrectly inferred as another construction using 'be'; for example, 'I be in my office by 7:30,' (habitual) could be inferred as 'I'll be in my office by 7:30' (future)." "Our research highlights the need for equitable ASR systems to take into account dialectal differences beyond acoustic modeling." — [Martin & Tang 2020](https://www.isca-archive.org/interspeech_2020/martin20_interspeech.pdf)
- Wassink, Gansen & Bartholomew (2022), "Uneven success: automatic speech recognition and ethnicity-related dialects", *Speech Communication* 140:50–70 **[PR]** (note: not *Journal of Phonetics*). They evaluate one ASR system on Pacific Northwest Native American, African American, European American and ChicanX speakers, and propose using sociophonetic knowledge to improve recognition. Seen only as abstract and bibliographic record; per-group figures not verified. — [dblp record](https://dblp1.uni-trier.de/rec/journals/speech/WassinkGB22.html)

### Inferences
- In bias studies, the reference itself is an editorial artefact. CORAAL keeps "we was" and "they kids"; Koenecke et al. then standardised some lexical spellings (aks → ask, gonna, tryna). Measured disparities therefore depend on what the evaluators chose to normalise. For the capstone: if a clean-style model (Whisper) turns "we was" into "we were", that is a style or editing behaviour that CORAAL's grammar-preserving conventions will count as an error. A WER gap on CORAAL may partly measure grammatical standardisation, not just mishearing. Martin & Tang give the mechanism; Koenecke et al. argue the acoustic side dominates overall. Both should be cited, together with the tension between them.
- Strongest to cite: Koenecke et al. 2020 (PNAS); Martin & Tang 2020 (Interspeech).

### Gaps
- I found no peer-reviewed paper that explicitly measures modern end-to-end models (Whisper, Parakeet) rewriting AAE grammar (e.g. "we was" → "we were") as a style effect. That would be the capstone's own contribution if shown.
- Wassink et al. full text not read.

## 6. "Whose transcript is ground truth?" (multiple references, semantic metrics, explicit critiques)

### Takeaway
A small but growing literature says outright that ASR references are constructed by convention, not discovered. McNamara et al. (Rev, 2024) operationalise this with verbatim and non-verbatim multi-references. Choi et al. (May 2026) argue that a single-convention "reference monism" is an epistemic injustice and propose reporting a WER range across conventions. Semantic metrics (SemDist) step around literal style differences altogether.

### Cited Findings
- Choi, Teleki, Caverlee, del Rio, Miller & Choi (8 May 2026), "Beyond Single Ground Truth: Reference Monism as Epistemic Injustice in ASR Evaluation", arXiv:2605.07084 **[PP]**, abstract only. "Ground truth transcripts are not discovered - they are produced by human annotators following conventions"; verbatim, non-verbatim and legal conventions "judge the same ASR output differently"; they propose "WER-Range" (reporting performance "across legitimate conventions rather than assuming a single correct answer") and an "Epistemic Injustice Distance". Applied to AphasiaBank. A search summary reported WER shifting "by nearly a factor of two" by convention: UNVERIFIED, not in the abstract I saw. Note that co-authors del Rio and Miller are Rev / Earnings-22 authors. — [Choi et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.07084)
- McNamara et al. 2024 **[PP]**: describe WER as "exact string equality against a ground truth reference" and propose multi-reference and GOLD WER (see Section 3). — [McNamara et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.07937)
- Kim, Arora, Le, Yeh, Fuegen, Kalinli & Seltzer (2021), "Semantic Distance: A New Metric for ASR Performance Analysis Towards Spoken Language Understanding", arXiv:2104.02138 (Interspeech 2021) **[PP/PR]**: WER measures "literal correctness instead of semantic correctness"; SemDist is the distance between RoBERTa embeddings of reference and hypothesis. — [Kim et al. 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2104.02138)
- Kim, Le, Zheng, Singh, Arora, Zhai, Fuegen, Kalinli & Seltzer (2022), "Evaluating User Perception of Speech Recognition System Quality with Semantic Distance Metric", arXiv:2110.05376 **[PP/PR]**, read full text: WER "sometimes correlates poorly with user perception/judgement of transcription quality … because WER weighs every word equally"; on 71K and 36K user-annotated outputs, SemDist correlates better with user perception than WER. — [Kim et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2110.05376)
- Glenn et al. 2010 use the term "Word Disagreement Rate" rather than error rate for transcriber-vs-transcriber comparisons "since not all of the transcription 'errors' are truly mistakes". This is an early explicit recognition that a single reference is not ground truth. — [Glenn et al. 2010](http://www.lrec-conf.org/proceedings/lrec2010/pdf/849_Paper.pdf)
- Radford et al. 2023: "systems that output transcripts that would be judged as correct by humans can still have a large WER due to minor formatting differences." — [Radford et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2212.04356)

### Inferences
- The capstone's framing ("court reporters vs engines; two human references differ by 13.4%") fits directly into this line. Choi et al. 2026 explicitly name a "legal" convention alongside verbatim and non-verbatim, which matches the court-reporter comparison. McNamara et al. provide the method: multi-reference or GOLD WER. Glenn et al.'s "word disagreement rate" gives a long-standing precedent for not calling human-human differences "errors".
- A semantic metric would show whether Whisper's clean-style deletions are "contentful" errors. Fillers and stutters should barely move SemDist, while habitual-be rewrites or hallucinations would. This is a hypothesis, untested here.
- Strongest to cite: McNamara et al. 2024 (method plus Earnings-22 data); Kim et al. 2021 (SemDist); Choi et al. 2026 as a recent, explicit normative argument, cited as a preprint.

### Gaps
- Choi et al. 2026 full text not read; the AphasiaBank numbers are unverified.
- I did not find peer-reviewed work comparing court-reporter (legal verbatim) transcripts against ASR under multiple reference conventions. Other researchers on this project may cover court-reporting standards.
