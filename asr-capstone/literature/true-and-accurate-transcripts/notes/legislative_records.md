## UK Hansard: the "full report" definition, editing practice, corrections, and scholarship (Slembrouck 1992; Mollin 2007)

### Takeaway
The UK Official Report has defined itself for over a century as "not strictly verbatim" but "substantially the verbatim report": repetitions and redundancies come out, obvious mistakes are corrected, and nothing that "adds to the meaning" is left out. Two peer-reviewed linguistic studies (Slembrouck 1992; Mollin 2007) show the editing goes further than that: it removes performance features and contextual talk, and pushes speakers' word and grammar choices toward more formal written forms.

### Cited Findings

**The definition (exact wording)**
- Full wording, as quoted (with citation to Erskine May) by British Columbia's Hansard Services, 2024 guide [OFFICIAL GUIDANCE, verified from PDF]: "The Official Report is a full report, in the first person, of all speakers alike, a full report being defined as one 'which, though not strictly verbatim, is substantially the verbatim report, with repetitions and redundancies omitted and with obvious mistakes corrected, but which on the other hand leaves out nothing that adds to the meaning of the speech or illustrates the argument.'" Cited there as Erskine May, 25th ed., para 7.19, p. 151; also House of Commons Procedure and Practice (Canada), 3rd ed., p. 270. — [BC Hansard Services, "Hansard Publications and Guidelines for Corrections by Members" (rev. 2024)](https://members.leg.bc.ca/wp-content/uploads/2024/10/Hansard-Publications-and-Guidelines-for-Corrections-by-Members-Revisions-2024.pdf)
- The Commonwealth Hansard Editors Association (CHEA) attributes the same wording to the 1907 Select Committee of the UK House of Commons: "which, though not strictly verbatim, is substantially the verbatim report, with repetitions and redundancies omitted and with obvious mistakes corrected, but which on the other hand leaves out nothing that adds to the meaning of the speech" [professional association, verified] — [CHEA, About Hansard](https://www.commonwealth-hansard.org/about-hansard.html)
- **Date conflict, flag it in the paper:** Wikipedia attributes the definition to an **1893** select committee, citing Ian Church, *Official Report [Hansard] Centenary Volume* (2009), p. xvi, and quotes it with an extra parenthesis: "a report which, though not strictly verbatim, is substantially the verbatim report with repetitions and redundancies omitted and with obvious mistakes (including grammatical mistakes) corrected" [WIKI, verified] — [Wikipedia, "Hansard"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hansard). CHEA, Zimbabwe's Parliament and others say 1907 — [Parliament of Zimbabwe](https://parlzim.gov.zw/?p=2059) (search excerpt). A 1908 Commons debate refers to an 1892/1893 Select Committee recommending a "full" report and to the printing contract that "ran out in June, 1907" — [HC Deb 14 May 1908 vol 188 cc1356-406](https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/commons/1908/may/14/class-ii) (search excerpt). The most likely reading (my inference, unverified against the committee report itself): the 1893 committee recommended a "full" report; the 1907 Select Committee on Parliamentary Debates wrote the definition, and the in-house Official Report began in 1909.
- Scottish Parliament restates it [OFFICIAL GUIDANCE]: "It is a 'substantially verbatim' report. This means that repetitions and redundancies are omitted and obvious mistakes are corrected." — [Scottish Parliament, About the Official Report](https://www.parliament.scot/chamber-and-committees/official-report/about-the-official-report) (search excerpt)
- The Clerk of the House of Commons, written evidence to the Procedure Committee inquiry "Correcting the record" (2023–24) [OFFICIAL, search excerpt only; page 403'd]: the Official Report "is not a verbatim transcript but rather a substantially verbatim report with repetitions and unnecessary words removed and obvious mistakes corrected. It does not omit, or insert, content that would alter the meaning of what has been said." — [Written evidence, Clerk of the House (CTR 06)](https://committees.parliament.uk/writtenevidence/111209/html/)

**What Hansard removes and corrects (practice)**
- BC Hansard (follows UK practice) spells it out [OFFICIAL GUIDANCE, verified]: "Hansard staff apply a light hand when making editorial changes, confining themselves to correcting grammar and syntax when this can be done with minimal changes to the Member's words. Repetitions, false starts, and self-corrections may be removed in the interests of clarity and readability, and obvious errors (i.e. those that an average listener would recognize, such as a mispronounced word) may be corrected, provided the error did not give rise to discussion in the House." — [BC Hansard guide 2024](https://members.leg.bc.ca/wp-content/uploads/2024/10/Hansard-Publications-and-Guidelines-for-Corrections-by-Members-Revisions-2024.pdf)
- A University of Westminster linguistics blog report on a visit to Commons Hansard (2020) [BLOG]: editors described the aim as making it read "not that one person *said* it, but that one person *wrote* it"; the author watched a sentence get reordered and given a connective ("therefore") for "well-formedness"; a sub-editor said contractions and informal language are now removed less strictly than in 1992; the author still saw "a sizeable gulf" between "substantially verbatim" and practice. It also quotes a peer (via Crewe 2010, ethnography of the Lords): "The ragged edges get smoothed over." — [Eagles, Univ. of Westminster linguistics blog](https://blog.westminster.ac.uk/linguistics-events/?p=291)

**Members' corrections (UK)**
- Backbench MPs have only "rapid self-correction of simple factual errors by clarification directly with Hansard or alternatively a clarificatory point of order after the fact"; there is a distinction "between silent corrections, corrections subject to the formal corrections process [ministers], and clarifications of meaning made through written ministerial statements" — [Clerk's written evidence CTR 06](https://committees.parliament.uk/writtenevidence/111209/html/) (search excerpt) [OFFICIAL]
- The Procedure Committee report "Correcting the record" (2024) and Full Fact's 2023 briefing pushed to extend a formal corrections scheme to all MPs, noting the Scottish Parliament already lets all MSPs correct the record — [Procedure Committee report](https://publications.parliament.uk/pa/cm5803/cmselect/cmproced/521/report.html); [Full Fact briefing 2023](https://fullfact.org/media/uploads/web_-_parliamentary_briefing_-_correcting_the_record_-_full_fact_-_2023.pdf); [Civil Service World, "MPs back new rules for fixing on-the-record mistakes"](https://www.civilserviceworld.com/professions/article/parliamentary-corrections-process-opened-up-to-mps) [JOURNALISM] (all search excerpts; outcome not verified)
- Scottish Parliament's correction rules [OFFICIAL GUIDANCE, verified]: "Only the person whose words were reported can ask for a review", within 20 working days; the Official Report will not make corrections that "change the meaning of what you said" or "add extra information"; changes are made consistent with "our remit to be substantially verbatim"; after 35 working days "Official Reports are finalised and cannot be changed again." — [Scottish Parliament, Corrections and changes to the Official Report](https://www.parliament.scot/chamber-and-committees/what-was-said-and-official-reports/corrections-and-changes-to-the-official-report)

**Slembrouck 1992 [PEER-REVIEWED]**
- Citation: Stef Slembrouck, "The parliamentary Hansard 'verbatim' report: the written construction of spoken discourse," *Language and Literature* 1(2): 101–119, 1992, doi:10.1177/096394709200100202 — [Benjamins bibliography entry](https://benjamins.com/online/ebop/publications/38287)
- Findings, as reported by later work (I could not read the article itself): spoken "anterior discourse" is transformed into a represented "reported discourse"; colloquialisms, regionally marked forms, false starts and repetition are removed; Hansard "privileges ideational utterances" so hedges and stance markers are often deleted; the edits change both interpersonal and ideational meaning — [Eagles blog summary](https://blog.westminster.ac.uk/linguistics-events/?p=291) [BLOG]; [Frontiers in Communication 2023 article citing it](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/communication/articles/10.3389/fcomm.2023.1047799/pdf) (search excerpt)

**Mollin 2007 [PEER-REVIEWED]**
- Citation: Sandra Mollin, "The Hansard hazard: gauging the accuracy of British parliamentary transcripts," *Corpora* 2(2): 187–210, Nov 2007 (online Oct 2008, so often cited as 2008), doi:10.3366/cor.2007.2.2.187; Crossref lists 78 citing works.
- Abstract, verbatim (from Crossref metadata): "Large databases of transcribed speech, downloadable from the Internet, are a corpus linguist's dream. They turn into a corpus linguist's nightmare, however, when the transcriptions are not linguistically accurate. In this paper I assess the suitability of the Hansard parliamentary transcripts (200 million words, downloadable) as a corpus linguistic resource, comparing a sample of the official transcript to a transcript made from a recording of a House of Commons session. The findings are that, as could be expected from earlier research, the transcripts omit performance characteristics of spoken language, such as incomplete utterances or hesitations, as well as any type of extrafactual, contextual talk (e.g., about turn-taking). Moreover, however, the transcribers and editors also alter speakers' lexical and grammatical choices towards more conservative and formal variants. Linguists ought, therefore, to be cautious in their use of the Hansard transcripts and, generally, in the use of transcriptions that have not been made for linguistic purposes." — [Crossref record](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.3366/cor.2007.2.2.187); [publisher page](https://www.euppublishing.com/doi/10.3366/cor.2007.2.2.187)
- Related later study: V. Michael Cribb and Shivani Rochford, "The Transcription and Representation of Spoken Political Discourse in the UK House of Commons," *International Journal of English Linguistics* 8(2): 1–14, 2018, doi:10.5539/ijel.v8n2p1 [PEER-REVIEWED, cited by Edwards 2023; not read] — [Edwards 2023, fn 5](https://www.aspg.org.au/wp-content/uploads/2023/09/Cecilia-Edwards-Curating-the-record.pdf)

### Inferences
- The 1907 definition is the clearest written statement anywhere that an official record is a judgement: it licenses removal ("repetitions and redundancies") and correction ("obvious mistakes") while setting a meaning-based limit ("leaves out nothing that adds to the meaning"). Deciding what "adds to the meaning" is the editor's call. This maps directly onto the ASR comparison: an ASR engine that drops disfluencies is doing what Hansard does on purpose.
- The "obvious to an average listener" test and the "provided the error did not give rise to discussion" carve-out (BC) are explicit editorial judgement rules with no counterpart in court-reporting "verbatim" rhetoric.
- Mollin's finding that editors shift lexis and grammar toward "more conservative and formal variants" is the strongest peer-reviewed evidence that the gap goes beyond disfluency removal.

## Other Westminster systems: Australia, Canada, New Zealand ("substantially verbatim")

### Takeaway
Australia, Canada and New Zealand all use the same "substantially verbatim" standard. Australia's mission statement is the most explicit: chamber speeches get a "readable" rendition, committee witnesses get a "basically verbatim" one, and "politically sensitive subjects require a more strictly verbatim approach", i.e. the degree of editing openly varies by context.

### Cited Findings
- **Australia, Hansard mission statement** [OFFICIAL GUIDANCE, verified]: "To provide an accurate, substantially verbatim account of the proceedings of the parliament and its committees which, while usually correcting obvious mistakes, neither adds to nor detracts from the meaning of the speech or the illustration of the argument." "For chambers: A rendition which is accurate and readable, with minimal alterations being made only to clarify ambiguous or confusing passages and to ensure that the meaning is understandable and the argument coherent. Politically sensitive subjects require a more strictly verbatim approach." "For committees: An accurate, basically verbatim rendition. Witnesses should, by and large, be given their exact words, even though these are sometimes confusing and not always strictly grammatical. Editing should be kept to a minimum and used only in circumstances where it is deemed absolutely necessary and can be justified as such." — [Parliament of Australia, Hansard](https://www.aph.gov.au/Parliamentary_Business/Hansard/Search)
- Australia, corrections: members may propose corrections to the proof Hansard, which becomes official about two weeks after the sitting week — [Parliamentary Education Office](https://peo.gov.au/understand-our-parliament/how-parliament-works/parliament-at-work/hansard) (search excerpt). *House of Representatives Practice* reportedly bars corrections that change the sense of what was said or add new matter, as quoted in a 2015 news story on a disputed change to the record — [The New Daily, "Australian Parliament record 'changed'" (2015)](https://thenewdaily.com.au/news/2015/03/17/australian-parliament-record-changed) [JOURNALISM; search excerpt; exact *House of Representatives Practice* wording not verified]
- **Canada (federal)**: the BC guide cites *House of Commons Procedure and Practice*, 3rd ed., p. 270 for the Erskine May definition, i.e. the federal Commons adopts it — [BC guide 2024](https://members.leg.bc.ca/wp-content/uploads/2024/10/Hansard-Publications-and-Guidelines-for-Corrections-by-Members-Revisions-2024.pdf). Canada was the first national parliament with directly employed official reporters (from 1880) — [CHEA](https://www.commonwealth-hansard.org/about-hansard.html) (search excerpt).
- **Canada (British Columbia), corrections rule** [OFFICIAL GUIDANCE, verified]: "Members should restrict themselves to reporting misheard words or other transcription errors and are not able to polish, improve or otherwise rewrite their speeches." All requests "are subject to approval by the Director of Hansard Services, in accordance with Hansard's substantially verbatim reporting guidelines." Standing Order 120: the transcript "shall be a 'substantially verbatim' report." — [BC guide 2024](https://members.leg.bc.ca/wp-content/uploads/2024/10/Hansard-Publications-and-Guidelines-for-Corrections-by-Members-Revisions-2024.pdf)
- **Canada (Manitoba)**: describes its transcript as "essentially verbatim" and removes stutters, among other changes — [Manitoba Hansard, About](https://gov.mb.ca/legislature/hansard/hansard_about.html) (search excerpt)
- **New Zealand**, historical/practitioner account [CONFERENCE PAPER by a NZ Hansard staffer and PhD researcher; Australasian Study of Parliament Group 2023]: "All of the characteristics of spoken language were trimmed from the report--the false starts, the stumbles, the repetitions, the excess verbiage, the broken syntax, and the slips of the tongue. But the combination of poor acoustics and considerable editorial licence opened the door to misinterpretation. Hence the convention of allowing members to check the draft of their speeches prior to publication, which sometimes raised questions about the integrity of Hansard as a true record of spoken proceedings." Also: members "did not have freedom to amend their draft Hansards however they pleased"; since 1896 a Hansard Supervisor handled change requests; video of debates "has greatly reduced the potential for clashes between members and Hansard staff ... over corrections". She prefers to call Hansard "a curated report" rather than an edited one. — [Cecilia Edwards, "Curating the record of free speech in Parliament," ASPG 2023](https://www.aspg.org.au/wp-content/uploads/2023/09/Cecilia-Edwards-Curating-the-record.pdf)

### Inferences
- Australia's chamber/committee split is a useful parallel to court reporting: witnesses (like courtroom testimony) get near-exact words; politicians' set speeches get a readable rendition. The rule "politically sensitive subjects require a more strictly verbatim approach" concedes that normal editing could alter meaning in ways that matter.
- Edwards's point that recordings and video reduced correction disputes suggests that once audio exists, the "verbatim" claim becomes checkable, which is exactly the position ASR puts court transcripts in.

## US Congressional Record: "substantially verbatim," revise and extend, bullets and typeface, Senate practice

### Takeaway
The statute (44 U.S.C. 901) and House rules require a "substantially verbatim" Record, but members may insert remarks never spoken. Since 1 March 1978 unspoken material is supposed to be flagged with a bullet; the House switched to a distinctive typeface in 1985–86, and in 1995 made "substantially verbatim account of remarks actually rendered" a House rule enforceable as a standard of conduct. The Senate still uses the bullet. A D.C. Circuit case and the 2006 Hamdan colloquy show the flags are not always applied.

### Cited Findings
- **Statute** [OFFICIAL RULE, verified]: 44 U.S.C. 901: "The Joint Committee on Printing shall control the arrangement and style of the Congressional Record, and while providing that it shall be substantially a verbatim report of proceedings, shall take all needed action for the reduction of unnecessary bulk." — [GPO, Laws and Rules for Publication of the Congressional Record](https://www.govinfo.gov/media/rules.txt)
- **Bullet rule (JCP rule 3)** [OFFICIAL RULE, verified]: "Only as an aid in distinguishing the manner of delivery in order to contribute to the historical accuracy of the Record, statements or insertions in the Record where no part of them was spoken will be preceded and followed by a 'bullet' symbol, i.e., <bullet>." — [GPO rules](https://www.govinfo.gov/media/rules.txt)
- **House supplemental rule 7** [OFFICIAL RULE, verified]: "the Congressional Record shall be a substantially verbatim account of remarks made during the proceedings of the House, subject only to technical, grammatical and typographical corrections authorized by the Member making the remarks involved. Unparliamentary remarks may be deleted only by permission or order of the House. Consistent with Rule 9 of the Joint Committee on Printing Rules, any revision shall consist only of technical, grammatical or typographical corrections of the original copy and shall not include deletions of correct material, substitutions for correct material or additions of new subject matter. By obtaining unanimous consent to revise and extend, a Member will be able to relax the otherwise strict prohibition ... only in two respects: (1) to revise by technical, grammatical and typographical corrections; and (2) to extend remarks in a distinctive type style to follow the remarks actually uttered. In no event would the actually uttered remarks be removable." — [GPO rules](https://www.govinfo.gov/media/rules.txt); same text in [House Rules and Manual (107th), §686, "effective August 12, 1986"](https://www.gpo.gov/fdsys/pkg/HMAN-107/html/HMAN-107-pg378.htm)
- **History, from the House Manual** [OFFICIAL, verified]: "The Joint Committee on Printing amended the rules for publication of the Record, effective March 1, 1978, to require the identification in the Record by 'bullet' symbols of statements or insertions no part of which were actually delivered in debate (Feb. 20, 1978, p. 3676). Where the House permitted all Members leave to revise and extend their remarks on a certain subject, those Members who actually spoke during the debate could revise their remarks to appear as if actually delivered, but Members' statements no part of which were spoken were preceded and followed by a 'bullet' symbol (Nov. 15, 1983, p. 32729). Then in the 99th Congress, the House adopted a resolution requesting the Joint Committee on Printing to adopt temporary rules to require distinctive type styles rather than bulleting of remarks not actually spoken in debate (H. Res. 230, July 31, 1985, p. 21783), and also adopted a resolution requesting that those rules be made permanent (H. Res. 514, Aug. 12, 1986, p. 20980)." — [House Manual §§ 686–689 (HMAN-107)](https://www.gpo.gov/fdsys/pkg/HMAN-107/html/HMAN-107-pg378.htm)
  - Note the loophole this records: before 1985, a member who spoke *any* part could revise the whole speech "to appear as if actually delivered"; the bullet only caught statements of which *no part* was spoken.
- **1995 rule** [OFFICIAL, verified]: §687 "Substantially verbatim account": "The requirement of rule 7 of the supplemental rules outlined above that the Congressional Record be a substantially verbatim account of remarks actually rendered was included as former clause 9 of rule XIV in the 104th Congress (current clause 8(a) of rule XVII), with the prescription that that rule constitute a standard of conduct ... (sec. 213, H. Res. 6, Jan. 4, 1995, p. 468). Clause 8(a) of rule XVII also applies to statements and rulings of the Chair (Jan. 20, 1995, p. 1866). Under that clause remarks actually delivered may not be deleted and remarks inserted must appear in distinctive type (Jan. 4, 1995, p. 541). The Speaker has instructed the Official Reporters of Debates to adhere strictly to the requirement ... (Mar. 2, 1988, p. 2963; Feb. 3, 1993, p. 1980)." — [House Manual §687](https://www.gpo.gov/fdsys/pkg/HMAN-107/html/HMAN-107-pg378.htm)
  - **Wording note:** the House rule says "substantially verbatim account"; "substantially a verbatim report" is the statute's wording. The phrase "substantially a verbatim account" in the brief does not match either exactly.
- **Current House Practice** [OFFICIAL, verified]: clause 8 makes the Record "a substantially verbatim account of the proceedings of the House"; remarks are "subject only to technical, grammatical, and typographical corrections authorized by the Member making the remarks involved"; "Substantive insertions submitted under leave to 'revise and extend' are printed in distinctive type"; "A speech that has been substantively revised is printed as delivered and then separately printed as revised in distinctive type"; "The House may not change the Congressional Record merely to show what should have been said on the floor"; a member may not insert matter that "alters the nature of colloquies as delivered on the floor or changes the meaning of what another Member said." — [House Practice (112th Cong.), ch. "Congressional Record"](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/GPO-HPRACTICE-112/html/GPO-HPRACTICE-112-16.htm); current edition [GPO-HPRACTICE-118](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/GPO-HPRACTICE-118/pdf/GPO-HPRACTICE-118-16.pdf)
- Clause 8(c) of rule XVII lets the Ethics Committee investigate compliance — [House Practice 118](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/GPO-HPRACTICE-118/pdf/GPO-HPRACTICE-118-16.pdf) (search excerpt)
- **Senate** [OFFICIAL GUIDANCE, verified]: "Remarks that are not actually spoken on the floor, but are inserted into the Record by a member of Congress, are indicated by a different typeface in the House portion, and are preceded by a bullet symbol in the Senate section. Inserted Senate statements unrelated to pending business are usually printed near the end of the Senate proceedings under the heading 'Additional Statements.' Undelivered House statements are usually printed in the 'Extensions of Remarks' section." The Record "differs from its predecessors in that it is a substantially verbatim account of the words spoken"; predecessors "mostly paraphrased." — [U.S. Senate, Congressional Record](https://www.senate.gov/pagelayout/legislative/one_item_and_teasers/Cong_record_fullstory_page.htm). Senate front matter today: the bullet "identifies statements or insertions which are not spoken by the Member of the Senate on the floor" — [Cong. Rec. Jan 23, 2025 front matter](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/CREC-2025-01-23/html/CREC-2025-01-23-pt1-PgS-FrontMatter-35.htm) (search excerpt)
- **CRS** [OFFICIAL GUIDANCE, verified]: "The Congressional Record is a substantially verbatim account of remarks made ..."; members ask permission to "revise and extend" (i.e., expand their remarks), and insertions "are distinguishable by a different type style. In the Senate, a bullet symbol precedes and distinguishes undelivered remarks." — [CRS, "A User's Guide to the Congressional Record" (98-265)](https://www.everycrsreport.com/reports/98-265.html)
- **GPO's role**: GPO's Director prints the Record "as furnished by the official reporters," and must hold manuscripts out for member revision to set deadlines (JCP rules 2, 4); House debate transcript "still out for revision" is due back to the Office of Official Reporters of Debates by 5 p.m. or 2 hours after adjournment — [GPO rules](https://www.govinfo.gov/media/rules.txt). The Record "is not, however, the official record, that function being fulfilled by the Journal" — [House Manual](https://www.gpo.gov/fdsys/pkg/HMAN-107/html/HMAN-107-pg378.htm)
- **Court case, *Gregg v. Barrett*, 771 F.2d 539 (D.C. Cir. 1985)** [COURT OPINION, verified]: members of Congress and others sued over an inaccurate Record; the court noted speeches printed as delivered: "Yet there are no bullets in the Record to indicated [sic] that any of these speeches were not delivered on the floor"; quoted an advisory committee: "[i]f, in the future, the leadership should desire a completely verbatim transcript such as is produced in a courtroom, it need only give the word and the changeover will be accomplished"; and held "There simply is no first amendment right to receive a verbatim transcript of the proceedings of Congress." (Dismissed as nonjusticiable / political question; my summary.) — [771 F.2d 539](https://m.openjurist.org/771/f2d/539)
  - The court's citation of "a completely verbatim transcript such as is produced in a courtroom" is a direct, citable contrast between legislative and court reporting norms.
- **Hamdan colloquy (2006)** [JOURNALISM, verified]: Senators Kyl and Graham cited, in an amicus brief in *Hamdan v. Rumsfeld*, a Senate "colloquy" on the Detainee Treatment Act that was inserted into the Record, not spoken; "the colloquy is scripted to sound as if it were live. At one point Kyle says, I see that we are nearing the end of our allotted time." Slate's Emily Bazelon: "the Congressional record is full of all kinds of things that didn't happen on the floor of Congress." — [NPR Day to Day / Slate, 28 Mar 2006](https://radio.wpsu.org/2006-03-28/slates-jurisprudence-senates-link-to-hamdan)
- **Older scholarship**: Howard N. Mantel, "The Congressional Record: Fact or Fiction of the Legislative Process," *Western Political Quarterly* 12(4), Dec 1959, doi:10.1177/106591295901200407 [PEER-REVIEWED]: under House practice a member "may insert into the Record as a speech material never uttered on the floor of the House"; quotes Sen. Richard Neuberger's 1958 NYT piece "The Congressional Record is Not a Record" on senators "virtually rewriting the speeches and retorts just delivered on the floor," and his S. Res. 168 (86th Cong.) to require remarks recorded "as actually made." — [vLex copy of Mantel 1959](https://law-journals-books.vlex.com/vid/the-congressional-record-fact-874230413)
- Secondary [library guide]: McGill library says since 1978 unspoken content is supposed to be marked, "though this rule is not always followed" — [McGill Library, US debates](https://mcgill.ca/library/find/govinfo/us/us-debates) (search excerpt)

### Inferences
- The US system is the opposite of Hansard's: it does little editing of spoken words (only "technical, grammatical and typographical" fixes) but allows wholesale *addition* of unspoken text, controlled only by a typographic flag. Both depart from "verbatim," in different directions.
- The 1983 precedent (any member who spoke any part could revise to "appear as if actually delivered") shows how much the "verbatim" claim depended on a narrow flagging rule.

### Gaps
- No empirical study found that measures what share of the Record is never spoken; I found no reliable percentage. Do not cite a figure.
- Did not verify the *Hamdan v. Rumsfeld*, 548 U.S. 557 (2006) majority footnote on the inserted colloquy (believed to exist; unverified here).
- Exact current text of House Rule XVII clause 8(a)–(c) was seen only as quoted in House Practice/Manual, not from the rules document itself.

## European Parliament (CRE) and UN verbatim records

### Takeaway
The European Parliament's verbatim report (CRE) is multilingual: each contribution appears in its original language, and speakers may correct their typescripts within a short window (one week, later five working days in the 2012 amendment). UN verbatim records print English speeches as delivered but other languages via the *interpretation*, and corrections from delegations are checked against the audio.

### Cited Findings
- **EP Rules, 2012 amendment of Rule 181 (verbatim reports)** [OFFICIAL RULE, search excerpt from the adopted text]: "A verbatim report of the proceedings of each sitting shall be drawn up as a multilingual document in which all oral contributions appear in their original language." "Speakers may make corrections to typescripts of their oral contributions within five working days. Corrections shall be sent within that deadline to the Secretariat." The multilingual report "and the English translation thereof shall be published as an annex to the Official Journal of the European Union." — [EP texts adopted TA-7-2012-0423](https://www.europarl.europa.eu/doceo/document/TA-7-2012-0423_EN.html)
- Earlier rule (Rule 173, 2007/08): "Speakers shall be required to return corrections to typescripts of their speeches to the Secretariat within one week." — [OEIL summary](https://oeil.europarl.europa.eu/oeil/en/document-summary?id=1012918) (search excerpt)
- **UN Security Council verbatim record, standard notice** [OFFICIAL, verified from S/PV.3721, 1996]: "This record contains the original text of speeches delivered in English and interpretations of speeches delivered in the other languages. The final text will be printed in the Official Records of the Security Council. Corrections should be submitted to original speeches only. They should be incorporated in a copy of the record and be sent under the signature of a member of the delegation concerned, within one week of the date of publication, to the Chief of the Verbatim Reporting Service" — [UN S/PV.3721](https://digitallibrary.un.org/record/229173/files/S_PV.3721-EN.pdf)
- General Assembly / Trusteeship Council records: corrections go to the Official Records Editing Section and "will be issued after the end of the session, in a separate corrigendum for each Committee" — [UN A/BUR/55/SR.1](https://digitallibrary.un.org/record/430796/files/A_BUR_55_SR.1-EN.pdf) (search excerpt)
- **UN Geneva** [OFFICIAL GUIDANCE, verified]: summary records are "not a verbatim record"; on corrections, "The correction is checked against the sound recording of the meeting to determine whether the record can be changed"; "Any corrections submitted will be duly checked against the audio recordings of the meeting." Delegates are told to give written texts in advance and "If you feel that a particular point or quotation should be placed on the record, say so." — [UN Geneva, Summary records FAQ](https://www.ungeneva.org/en/faqs/how-can-i-ensure-my-contribution-meeting-properly-reflected-record)

### Inferences
- The UN notice is a striking admission that the "verbatim" record of a non-English speech is in fact the interpreter's rendering, a layer of judgement before any editing.
- UN Geneva's rule that corrections are checked "against the audio recordings" makes the recording, not the transcript, the authority, a useful contrast with court practice where the certified transcript is the record.

## Studies comparing audio with official legislative transcripts

### Takeaway
Besides Mollin (UK), the best quantitative study found is Kawahara 2024: about 13% of words differ between faithful transcripts and official records in the Japanese Diet and over 20% in the European Parliament (English), and Diet editing fell about 40% between 2007 and 2016 after ASR was introduced.

### Cited Findings
- **Kawahara 2024** [CONFERENCE PAPER, peer-reviewed workshop; verified from PDF]: Tatsuya Kawahara, "Quantitative Analysis of Editing in Transcription Process in Japanese and European Parliaments and its Diachronic Changes," *ParlaCLARIN IV @ LREC-COLING 2024*, pp. 66–69. Findings: "% of edited words" between faithful transcript and official record is "approximately 13% in the Japanese Diet, while it is over 20% in the European Parliament" (Table 1: 12.9% vs 20.5%); "The larger difference in the European Parliament is attributed to grammatical" corrections for English; in the Diet "A large majority (93%) of edits ... are simple and can be classified as deletion, insertion, or substitution (correction) of words. Almost 90% of them are deletions, and almost half of them involve filler removal"; "there are much more complex corrections in the European Parliament because English needs many grammatical corrections and syntax reordering"; "The ratio of edits has been reduced by 40% over the ten years" (2007–2016); causes include "the deployment of the ASR system since 2011. Reporters now edit a faithful transcript, which is generated by the ASR system. In the old system based on stenography, they typed in text with editing in mind"; and "there is no written guideline in the Japanese Diet." Diet ASR character correct rate ~91%, rising to ~95% with deep learning in 2020. — [ACL Anthology 2024.parlaclarin-1.10](https://aclanthology.org/2024.parlaclarin-1.10) ([PDF](https://aclanthology.org/2024.parlaclarin-1.10.pdf))
- Parliamentary-corpus guide (Slovenia, ParlaMint/ SIDIH): "officially released records of parliamentary debates are not 100% verbatim transcriptions"; editing guidelines "are mostly not made public" — [SIDIH, ch. 4 "Parliamentary records"](https://sidih.si/cdn/121/ch4.html) [BOOK/online handbook; search excerpt]
- Finland: Voutilainen (2023) examines editorial changes in Finnish plenary transcripts, including "editing out of self-repairs, planning expressions, stuttering and slips-of-tongue" — [Frontiers in Communication 2023](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fcomm.2023.1047799) [PEER-REVIEWED; search excerpt; authorship of this article not confirmed]
- Iceland (Althingi): two-stage workflow; "It is common that an editor corrects transcription errors, fixes grammar or enriches the text with context" — ["Manual Post-editing of Automatically Transcribed Speeches from the Icelandic Parliament," arXiv 1807.11893](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1807.11893) [CONFERENCE/PREPRINT; search excerpt]
- Korhonen, Kotze & Tyrkkö (eds.), *Exploring Language and Society with Big Data: Parliamentary Discourse across Time and Space* (Benjamins, SCL 111, 2023) [BOOK]: includes work on colloquialization of the British record and cites Mollin — [Benjamins](https://lwc1.benjamins.com/catalog/scl.111.03jef)
- Bayley (ed.), *Cross-Cultural Perspectives on Parliamentary Discourse* (Benjamins, 2004), doi:10.1075/dapsac.10 [BOOK]: cited by Mollin 2007 — [Crossref reference list](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.3366/cor.2007.2.2.187)

### Inferences
- Kawahara's ASR finding is directly relevant: once reporters start from a faithful machine transcript rather than shorthand, the record became *more* verbatim, so the technology changes the editorial norm, not just the speed.
- A 13–20% word difference between "official" and "faithful" transcripts is far above the word error rates usually reported for good ASR, so official legislative records cannot be treated as ground truth for ASR scoring without adjustment (Kawahara counts only substitutions and deletions for that reason).

- No study found on the Irish Oireachtas record versus audio.
- Could not find Cucchi's work on parliamentary verbatim reports in this pass (Costanza Cucchi has written on European Parliament discourse; specific transcript-accuracy study not located).
- Bayley 2004 contents on transcription not checked.

## Professional associations: Intersteno, Hansard Association of Canada, CHEA

### Takeaway
CHEA publishes the 1907 definition as the foundation of Hansard reporting and frames Hansard around "integrity, impartiality, and excellence." I found no written editing principles from Intersteno or the Hansard Association of Canada.

### Cited Findings
- CHEA (Commonwealth Hansard Editors Association): quotes the 1907 definition (above); associates the Hansard name with "integrity, impartiality, and excellence in parliamentary reporting"; has regional groups (Africa, Asia-Indian Ocean, Australasia-Pacific, Canada, Caribbean-South Atlantic, Europe) — [CHEA, About Hansard](https://www.commonwealth-hansard.org/about-hansard.html) [professional association, verified]
- Hansard's "hallmarks are impartiality, accuracy, timeliness, readability, and accessibility" (Edwards, citing Verrier 2007, *Australasian Parliamentary Review*) — [Edwards 2023](https://www.aspg.org.au/wp-content/uploads/2023/09/Cecilia-Edwards-Curating-the-record.pdf)
- Australasia and Pacific Hansard Editors Association held its first conference in Melbourne in 1974 — [Edwards 2023, fn 33](https://www.aspg.org.au/wp-content/uploads/2023/09/Cecilia-Edwards-Curating-the-record.pdf)

### Inferences
- The professional bodies treat "readability" as a core value alongside "accuracy", stating openly that the two trade off, unlike court reporting's single "verbatim" ideal.

### Gaps
- Intersteno (the international federation of stenographers and parliamentary reporters): one search turned up no stated editing principles; not searched further.
- Hansard Association of Canada: no document found.
