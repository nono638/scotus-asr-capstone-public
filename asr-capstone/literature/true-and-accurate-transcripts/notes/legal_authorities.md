Research notes, 3 November 2026. Source-type tags: [STATUTE], [COURT RULE / REGULATION], [JUDICIARY POLICY], [CASE], [PROFESSIONAL CODE], [PRACTITIONER GUIDANCE], [OFFICIAL WEBSITE], [PEER-REVIEWED STUDY], [SEARCH SUMMARY ONLY - NOT VERIFIED].
"Verified" below means the quoted text was pulled from the page or PDF itself (downloaded and text-extracted), not from a search snippet.

## Q1. Federal: 28 U.S.C. § 753(b), Guide to Judiciary Policy Vol. 6, Judicial Conference policy

### Takeaway
Federal law sets the standard at the level of *words*: the statute requires proceedings to be "recorded verbatim", and the Judicial Conference's binding transcript format (Guide to Judiciary Policy, Vol. 6, Ch. 5, § 520) requires "all words and other verbal expressions uttered", with grammatical errors and misstatements "transcribed as spoken". It contains one explicit readability exception: false starts, stutters, "uhms and ahs" and verbal tics are "not normally included", but must be kept "whenever their exclusion could change a statement's meaning". The certified transcript is only "prima facie" correct, so it can be rebutted.

### Cited Findings
- [STATUTE, verified] 28 U.S.C. § 753(b), first sentence: "Each session of the court and every other proceeding designated by rule or order of the court or by one of the judges shall be recorded verbatim by shorthand, mechanical means, electronic sound recording, or any other method, subject to regulations promulgated by the Judicial Conference and subject to the discretion and approval of the judge. The regulations promulgated pursuant to the preceding sentence shall prescribe the types of electronic sound recording or other means which may be used." — [28 U.S.C. § 753, LII](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/28/753)
- [STATUTE, verified] § 753(b), on what must be recorded: "(1) all proceedings in criminal cases had in open court; (2) all proceedings in other cases had in open court unless the parties with the approval of the judge shall agree specifically to the contrary; and (3) such other proceedings as a judge of the court may direct..." — [LII](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/28/753)
- [STATUTE, verified] § 753(b), the evidentiary weight of the certificate: "The transcript in any case certified by the reporter or other individual designated to produce the record shall be deemed prima facie a correct statement of the testimony taken and proceedings had. No transcripts of the proceedings of the court shall be considered as official except those made from the records certified by the reporter or other individual designated to produce the record." — [LII](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/28/753)
- [STATUTE, verified] § 753(b) puts the "reporter or other individual designated to produce the record" on the same footing; it "shall attach his official certificate to the original shorthand notes or other original records so taken and promptly file them with the clerk who shall preserve them in the public records of the court for not less than ten years." § 753(c): "The reporters shall be subject to the supervision of the appointing court and the Judicial Conference in the performance of their duties". — [LII](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/28/753)

- [JUDICIARY POLICY, verified] § 520.40.10(a) "Striking of Portions of the Proceeding": "No portion of the proceeding must be omitted from the record by an order to strike. - [COURT RULE, verified] FRAP 10(e) is the federal mechanism for disputes over what was said: "(1) If any difference arises about whether the record truly discloses what occurred in the district court, the difference must be submitted to and settled by that court and the record conformed accordingly. (2) If anything material to either party is omitted from or misstated in the record by error or accident, the omission or misstatement may be corrected and a supplemental record may be certified and forwarded: (A) on stipulation of the parties; (B) by the district court before or after the record has been forwarded; or (C) by the court of appeals." — [FRAP 10, LII](https://www.law.cornell.edu/rules/frap/rule_10)

### Inferences
- The federal rule draws the line at meaning, not at words. Grammar and misstatements stay "as spoken" with no exception, but disfluencies (false starts, "uh") are left out by default and kept only when they bear on meaning. A speech engine that writes "clean verbatim" (drops fillers, keeps the wrong word) is close to the federal default. One that "fixes" grammar or a misspoken word breaks § 520.40.10(b)(1).
- The rule asks the reporter to judge whether a filler "could change a statement's meaning". That is a judgment about meaning. So even the federal verbatim standard is not purely mechanical, which is useful for a paper comparing human and machine transcripts.
- "Prima facie a correct statement" plus FRAP 10(e) means the law treats the certified transcript as a presumption, not as the truth. The recording, or the judge's settlement of the record, can override it.
- Strongest federal citations: 28 U.S.C. § 753(b) (statute) and Guide to Judiciary Policy Vol. 6, Ch. 5, § 520.40.10 (binding Judicial Conference policy). Cite the Guide by section number. The copies above are court-hosted reproductions, because the master PDF on uscourts.gov would not download.

## Q2. New York (and California, Texas, New Jersey, Washington, Illinois): duties, certification wording, settling transcripts, digital recording

### Takeaway
New York's statute speaks of the stenographer taking "full stenographic notes of the testimony and of all other proceedings" and furnishing a "certified transcript". It does not use the word "verbatim" in the sections checked. Accuracy disputes in civil appeals are settled under CPLR 5525(c), where the parties propose amendments and the trial judge settles the transcript. California and Texas use "correct" and "prima facie evidence" wording. Texas and New Jersey copy the federal "transcribed as spoken" rule, but without the federal exception for false starts.

### Inferences
- In New York the official record is tied to the stenographer's *notes* (§ 292: the notes "are part of the proceedings in the cause"). The transcript is a certified writing-out of those notes, and the trial judge has the final word on accuracy through CPLR 5525 settlement. This is a strong point for the paper: in NY the authority over "what was said" sits with the judge settling the transcript, not with the reporter alone.
- The "to the best of my ability" wording in Washington and in the federal transcriber certificate openly admits that transcription made from a recording is uncertain. The stenographer's certificate ("correct transcript") makes no such admission.
- Washington's "including any changes made by the trial judge reviewing the transcript" shows the judge's power to change a transcript written into the certificate itself. That runs against NCRA Advisory Opinion 4 (Q3), which says the reporter should not change the record at the judge's request alone.
- Strongest citations: N.Y. Judiciary Law §§ 292, 295, 300; CPLR 5525(c); Cal. CCP §§ 269, 273; Texas UFM § 3.15. All are primary law.

## Q3. NCRA Code of Professional Ethics and Advisory Opinions; practitioner texts on "verbatim"

### Takeaway
The NCRA Code itself does not define "verbatim" or ban editing in so many words. The working rules are in the Committee on Professional Ethics (COPE) Advisory Opinions. AO 4 says a reporter must not change the record, "even those of a grammatical nature", at a judge's request unless all counsel agree, and not at one party's request without a court order or the written agreement of all parties. AO 19 and AO 34 say the certificate is a statement that "the transcript is a true and accurate record of the proceeding". AO 31 and AO 47 say the reporter's job is "to preserve the spoken word on the record" and the transcript is "the written record of what the reporter heard and reported".

### Cited Findings
- [PROFESSIONAL CODE, verified] NCRA Code of Professional Ethics: "A Member Shall: Be fair and impartial toward each participant in all aspects of reported proceedings ... Guard against not only the fact but the appearance of impropriety. ... Be truthful and accurate when making public statements or when advertising the Member's qualifications or the services provided. ... Maintain the integrity of the reporting profession." (No provision mentions verbatim, grammar or editing.) — [NCRA Code of Professional Ethics](https://www.ncra.org/home/the-profession/NCRA-Code-of-Professional-Ethics)
- [PROFESSIONAL CODE - ADVISORY OPINION, verified] NCRA AO 4, "Altering the official record" (1987, revised 2013). Facts: a judge "directs the reporter which portions of the record, which were reported accurately, are to be deleted or altered"; counsel want their own remarks changed. Discussion: "In Scenario 1, the reporter should not make a change even if requested by the court. If the Court wishes to make any changes, even those of a grammatical nature, it should confer with all counsel. If they agree to the change, the reporter may then make it. In Scenario 2, counsel also may request that changes be made in the record, but this must be done by motion to the court. In Scenario 3, parties may make changes to the transcript by agreement of all parties in writing or by ruling of the court." Conclusion: "to alter the record solely upon the Court's request would be a violation of Provisions 1, 3, and 9". — [NCRA Advisory Opinion 4](https://ncra.org/home/the-profession/NCRA-Code-of-Professional-Ethics/COPE-Advisory-Opinions/group-page-(advisory-opinions)/Advisory-Opinion-4)
- [ADVISORY OPINION, verified] NCRA AO 19 (combined with No. 37, March 2014): "Most jurisdictions require the typical certification page to essentially state that (1) the deponent was duly sworn by the reporter; (2) the transcript is a true and accurate record of the proceeding; and (3) the person whose signature appears on the certificate is the one who reported and transcribed the proceeding." Conclusion: "The Public and the legal community must have absolute faith in the reporter's certification that the record is true and accurate. No act could undermine the profession more than false certification of a record." — [NCRA Advisory Opinion 19](https://ncra.org/home/the-profession/NCRA-Code-of-Professional-Ethics/COPE-Advisory-Opinions/group-page-(advisory-opinions)/Advisory-Opinion-19)
- [ADVISORY OPINION, verified] NCRA AO 34 (1998): "A reporter should not certify that a transcript is a true and accurate record of the proceeding if the transcript has not been personally produced by that reporter (or under that reporter's direction and control) or verified by that reporter." — [NCRA Advisory Opinion 34](https://ncra.org/home/the-profession/NCRA-Code-of-Professional-Ethics/COPE-Advisory-Opinions/group-page-(advisory-opinions)/Advisory-Opinion-34)
- [ADVISORY OPINION, verified] NCRA AO 31 (1996, revised April 2015), on describing gestures: "The role of a court reporter in reporting a proceeding is to preserve the spoken word on the record and not to function as a factual witness for one party to the proceeding." Describing nonverbal interaction at one side's request violates Provisions 1, 2 and 3. — [NCRA Advisory Opinion 31](https://ncra.org/home/the-profession/NCRA-Code-of-Professional-Ethics/COPE-Advisory-Opinions/group-page-(advisory-opinions)/Advisory-Opinion-31)
- [ADVISORY OPINION, verified] NCRA AO 47 (2015), on remarks caught by video microphones but not heard by the reporter: "The official transcript prepared by a reporter is the written record of what the reporter heard and reported at the proceeding. This is the fundamental aspect of the stenographic court reporter. A reporter should not include conversations unless they were actually heard and reported by the reporter." If all parties agree, the reporter may add them "provided the transcript clearly shows that the conversation is an excerpt from a video and not originally reported by the reporter". — [NCRA Advisory Opinion 47](https://ncra.org/home/the-profession/NCRA-Code-of-Professional-Ethics/COPE-Advisory-Opinions/group-page-(advisory-opinions)/Advisory-Opinion-47)
- [ADVISORY OPINION, verified] Every AO ends: "THIS PUBLIC ADVISORY OPINION REFLECTS THE STATUS OF THE LAW IN MOST JURISDICTIONS. MEMBERS ARE REQUIRED TO CONFORM TO THE ACCEPTED PRACTICES SET FORTH IN THIS PUBLIC ADVISORY OPINION TO THE EXTENT THAT SUCH PRACTICES ARE CONSISTENT WITH THEIR OWN APPLICABLE STATE AND LOCAL LAWS, RULES AND REGULATIONS." — [e.g. AO 4](https://ncra.org/home/the-profession/NCRA-Code-of-Professional-Ethics/COPE-Advisory-Opinions/group-page-(advisory-opinions)/Advisory-Opinion-4)
- [ADVISORY OPINION LIST, verified] Current opinions on the NCRA site: 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 8, 9, 10, 15, 17, 19, 20, 22, 23, 24, 25, 26, 28, 29, 31, 32, 34, 35, 36, 39, 43, 44, 45, 46, 47. None of the titles is about filler words, false starts or "verbatim" style. AO 4 is the only one on editing. — [NCRA COPE Advisory Opinions](https://ncra.org/home/the-profession/NCRA-Code-of-Professional-Ethics/COPE-Advisory-Opinions/group-page-(advisory-opinions)/Advisory-Opinion-34)
- [PEER-REVIEWED STUDY, verified] Hennink & Weber (2013), *Qualitative Health Research* 23(5): comparing court reporters with transcriptionists on 16 focus-group transcripts, "Court reporters made more errors, particularly in the omission of topical content and contextual detail and were less able to produce a verbatim transcript". Their error codebook includes "grammatical changes, where colloquial language, repeated words and false starts were grammatically corrected", with the example of "umm, well, people know, most people know that smoking's gonna kill you, but diabetes ain't so obvious" transcribed as "most people know that smoking is going to kill you, but diabetes is not so obvious." — [Hennink & Weber 2013, PMC4465445](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4465445/)

### Inferences
- NCRA puts the ethical weight on *who* may authorize a change (judge plus all counsel, or a court order). It does not set a word-level style rule. The word-level rule ("transcribed as spoken") comes from court format rules (federal § 520, Texas, NJ).
- AO 4's phrase "even those of a grammatical nature" is the clearest professional-code statement that a reporter may not quietly fix grammar, even at a judge's request. It is the best single citation for "reporters must not clean up testimony".
- AO 47's "what the reporter heard and reported" defines the record by human perception, not by an audio file. An ASR system or an audio recording can "hear" things the reporter did not, and NCRA's answer is to keep them out unless all parties agree and the source is labelled.
- Hennink & Weber is not legal authority. It is the only empirical source found showing that court reporters, in practice, do correct grammar and drop false starts (in a non-court setting).

### Gaps
- The practitioner texts named in the brief (Margie Wakeman Wells, *Court Reporting: Bad Grammar/Good Punctuation*; *Morson's English Guide for Court Reporters*; Mary Knapp's work) are print books. I did not access them and have no verified quotes. Their positions are NOT VERIFIED here; the author (an RPR) likely owns or can reach them.
- NCRA's "Guidelines for Professional Practice" page was downloaded but not checked for verbatim or editing language.
- I found no official NCRA definition of "verbatim". A 2015 JCR item says Advisory Opinions 1–44 were reviewed, and some were revised or rescinded; any rescinded opinion that dealt with editing could not be checked.

## Q4. Case law: omissions, "substantially verbatim", corrected grammar, settling the record

### Takeaway
The leading federal line treats § 753(b) as mandatory, but an incomplete transcript is not reversible in itself. Under *United States v. Selva*, 559 F.2d 1303 (5th Cir. 1977), new appellate counsel need only show that "a substantial and significant portion of the record" is missing. I found no reported appellate decision turning on a reporter correcting grammar or recording "what was meant". Disputes of that kind are handled as record-settlement matters (FRAP 10(e), CPLR 5525), not as reversible error.

### Cited Findings
- [CASE, verified from opinion text] *United States v. Selva*, 559 F.2d 1303 (5th Cir. 1977) (Tjoflat, J.), quoting § 753(b): "This language is clear, and its requirements are mandatory." "It is also established beyond any shadow of doubt that a criminal defendant has a right to a record on appeal which includes a complete transcript of the proceedings at trial. Hardy v. United States, 375 U.S. 277 (1964)." — [Selva, 559 F.2d 1303](https://m.openjurist.org/559/f2d/1303)
- [CASE, verified] *Selva*, describing two lines of cases: "The first holds that failure to comply with the Act is not error per se and will not work a reversal absent a specific showing of prejudice". But: "When, as here, a criminal defendant is represented on appeal by counsel other than the attorney at trial, the absence of a substantial and significant portion of the record, even absent any showing of specific prejudice or error, is sufficient to mandate reversal." (The missing portion was the prosecutor's closing argument.) — [Selva](https://m.openjurist.org/559/f2d/1303)
- [CASE, search summary] Later treatment: the Eleventh Circuit treats Selva as binding precedent from the former Fifth Circuit; some other circuits and later Fifth Circuit panels have declined to follow parts of it. Not individually verified. — [11th Cir. unpublished opinion citing Selva](https://media.ca11.uscourts.gov/opinions/unpub/files/200712918.pdf); [U.S. v. Selva summary, vLex](https://case-law.vlex.com/vid/u-s-v-selva-893549503)
- [CASE] *Hardy v. United States*, 375 U.S. 277 (1964): cited in Selva for the right to a complete transcript on appeal. (I read the citation in Selva, not the Hardy opinion itself.) — [Selva](https://m.openjurist.org/559/f2d/1303)
- [COURT RULE] FRAP 10(e) and CPLR 5525(c) (quoted in Q1 and Q2) are the procedures by which a disputed wording is settled by the trial judge. — [FRAP 10](https://www.law.cornell.edu/rules/frap/rule_10); [CPLR 5525](https://newyork.public.law/laws/n.y._civil_practice_law_and_rules_section_5525)

### Inferences
- Courts look at whether something is *missing* (substantial and significant omissions). They do not look at whether the wording is exactly verbatim. A transcript that tidies grammar but keeps the substance would almost certainly pass the *Selva* test, so the legal floor is lower than the professional "transcribed as spoken" standard.
- For the paper, the stronger legal hook for "meaning versus words" is the federal § 520.40.10(b)(2) test ("could change a statement's meaning"). Case law mainly gives the remedy side (settling the record, prejudice).

### Gaps
- *United States v. Huet-Vasquez* (named in the brief) was not located or verified. I cannot confirm its court, citation or holding.
- No case was found in which an appeal turned on a reporter correcting grammar or recording what a witness meant. A wider search (Westlaw/Lexis; terms like "court reporter" /s "grammar" /s transcript) may turn one up. I found no reliable source for such a case.
- No case on errors in *electronic* recordings (inaudible gaps in ESR or FTR) was verified in this pass.

## Q5. Court interpreter codes: complete and accurate rendering, hesitations, register

### Takeaway
Interpreter codes take the opposite position from the transcript format rules on disfluency. The NCSC Model Code, Canon 1, requires a rendering "without altering, omitting, or adding anything to what is stated". Its commentary requires interpreters to preserve "the style or register of speech" and to interpret even "rambling, or incoherent" statements, "This includes apparent misstatements". It also rejects word-for-word rendering "when they distort the meaning". Washington's adaptation adds "false starts" explicitly.

### Cited Findings
- [PROFESSIONAL CODE (model), verified] NCSC, *Court Interpretation: Model Guides for Policy and Practice in the State Courts* (William E. Hewitt, 1995), Chapter 9, Model Code of Professional Responsibility for Interpreters in the Judiciary, Canon 1: "CANON 1: ACCURACY AND COMPLETENESS. Interpreters shall render a complete and accurate interpretation or sight translation, without altering, omitting, or adding anything to what is stated or written, and without explanation." — [Model Code, Ch. 9 (Delaware Courts copy)](https://www.courts.delaware.gov/forms/download.aspx?id=134438)
- [PROFESSIONAL CODE (model), verified] Canon 1 Commentary: "The interpreter has a twofold duty: 1) to ensure that the proceedings in English reflect precisely what was said by a non-English speaking person, and 2) to place the non-English speaking person on an equal footing with those who understand English. This creates an obligation to conserve every element of information contained in a source language communication when it is rendered in the target language. Therefore, interpreters are obligated to apply their best skills and judgment to preserve faithfully the meaning of what is said in court, including the style or register of speech. Verbatim, 'word for word,' or literal oral interpretations are not appropriate when they distort the meaning of the source language, but every spoken statement, even if it appears non-responsive, obscene, rambling, or incoherent should be interpreted. This includes apparent misstatements. Interpreters should never interject their own words, phrases, or expressions." — [Model Code, Ch. 9](https://www.courts.delaware.gov/forms/download.aspx?id=134438)
- [COURT RULE (state adaptation), verified] Washington GR 11.2(1) and comment [2]: "Interpreters must reproduce in the target language the closest natural equivalent of the source language message without altering it by means of addition, omission, or explanation." ... "preserving the style and register of speech, and the ambiguities and nuances of the source statement. Everything must be interpreted, even if it appears nonresponsive, obscene, rambling, or incoherent. This includes false starts and apparent misstatements. However, verbatim, 'word for word,' or literal interpretation is inappropriate if it distorts the meaning of what is said or signed." Comment [4]: "The obligation to preserve accuracy includes the interpreter's duty to correct any substantive errors of interpretation as soon as possible." — [Wash. GR 11.2](https://www.courts.wa.gov/court_rules/pdf/GR/GA_GR_11_02_00.pdf)

### Inferences
- The brief's guessed wording ("including hesitations, false starts, repetitions") is NOT in the NCSC Model Code. The model says "rambling, or incoherent ... This includes apparent misstatements". "False starts" appears in Washington's adaptation, and "hesitations" was not found in either text. Quote these carefully.
- The contrast for the paper: the interpreter standard is explicitly *meaning-first* ("closest natural equivalent"; literal rendering is wrong if it distorts meaning) but also *register-preserving* (incoherence and misstatements kept). The federal transcript standard is *word-first* for grammar and misstatements but *meaning-gated* for disfluencies. Both systems tell the professional to keep the speaker's errors and to judge meaning.
- Strongest citation: the NCSC Model Code, Canon 1 and commentary (via the Hewitt 1995 Model Guides), plus a state rule that adopts it (Wash. GR 11.2).

### Gaps
- The NAJIT Code of Ethics PDF URL I tried returned an HTML stub, not the code. NAJIT's wording is not verified.
- The 1995 NCSC original was read through a Delaware Courts reproduction, not an ncsc.org copy (the ncsc.org PDF link returned 404).
- New York's own court interpreter rules (22 NYCRR Part 217 / UCS Court Interpreter Manual and Code of Ethics) were not checked.

## Q6. US Supreme Court oral argument transcripts

### Takeaway
SCOTUS transcripts are made by a private contract court reporting firm (Heritage Reporting Corporation now; Alderson Reporting earlier). The Court calls same-day transcripts "official but subject to final review". In 2004 the Court changed its practice to name the justice asking each question, explicitly "in the interest of the accuracy and completeness of the transcripts". No published corrections policy was found.

### Cited Findings
- [OFFICIAL WEBSITE, verified] Supreme Court argument-transcripts page: "Heritage Reporting Corporation provides the oral argument transcripts that are posted on this website on the same day an argument is heard by the Supreme Court. Same-day transcripts are considered official but subject to final review. Transcripts for oral arguments prior to October Term 2000 have been scanned from the Supreme Court Library collection." Bound copies "from October Term 2017 forward are available for purchase through Heritage Reporting Corporation"; for earlier terms, "contact the reporting company listed on the title page of the individual transcript". — [supremecourt.gov Argument Transcripts](https://www.supremecourt.gov/oral_arguments/argument_transcript/2022)
- [OFFICIAL PRESS RELEASE, verified] Supreme Court press release, 28 September 2004: "Beginning with the October Term 2004, the official transcripts of oral arguments will identify the Justices who ask questions during courtroom sessions. The change in practice is being made in the interest of the accuracy and completeness of the transcripts for reporting, research and archival purposes. Since 1968, when transcripts were first provided on a regular basis for the Court by private reporting companies, transcripts have identified a question from the Bench not by the justice but by using the word 'Question.' Prior to 1968, official transcripts were not prepared for the Court on a regular basis. Counsel who wished to have transcripts could make arrangements directly with private shorthand reporters, who often included the Justices' names." Transcripts "may be purchased ... directly from Alderson Reporting Service". — [SCOTUS press release, Sept. 28, 2004](https://www.supremecourt.gov/publicinfo/press/pressreleases/pr_09-28-04)
- [SECONDARY, search summary only] Oyez, an academic project, aligns Court audio with transcripts and did its own speaker identification for pre-2004 arguments. A research article notes Oyez identified instances of Justice Thomas speaking before 2004. Not verified from primary pages. — [Yale Law Journal, "Citing the Transcript of Oral Argument"](https://yalelawjournal.org/article/citing-the-transcript-of-oral-argument-which-justices-do-it-and-why); [Cornell ConvoKit Supreme Court corpus](https://convokit.cornell.edu/documentation/supreme.html)

### Inferences
- "Official but subject to final review" means even the highest court treats the first transcript as provisional. That matches the "prima facie" wording in § 753(b) and CCP § 273.
- For an ASR study using SCOTUS audio, the reference text is a professional reporter's product made under contract, and the Court says it is subject to revision. It should be described as a *reference*, not as ground truth.

### Gaps
- No documented SCOTUS policy on what "final review" changes, whether transcripts drop fillers or false starts, or how errors are corrected. I found no reliable source on this.
- The handover from Alderson to Heritage (the year and the contract) was not verified. The page implies Heritage from OT2017 for bound copies.
- Oyez's own statement of how it relates to the official transcripts was not read directly.

## Q7. Digital recording standards bodies (AAERT) and "verbatim" for electronic transcribers

### Takeaway
AAERT certifies electronic transcribers (CET) on the premise that they make the record "verbatim". Its exam blueprint tests "tics and mannerisms vs. substantive words" and "the importance of transcribing verbatim", and says the practical exam follows federal formatting. In practice, AAERT points to the federal § 520 standard rather than setting its own published definition of verbatim.

### Cited Findings
- [PROFESSIONAL CERTIFICATION STANDARD, verified] AAERT, "Table of Specifications (Blueprint) for the Certified Electronic Transcriber (CET) Examination" (updated 29 June 2026): "The Certified Electronic Transcriber captures the record by transcribing digitally recorded court and non-court proceedings verbatim." Tested items include "10301. Describe the role of a transcriptionist as an impartial non-party to the case"; "10302. Define the importance of an accurate and verbatim record and how it impacts judicial system"; "20101. Describe the difference between Proofreading Versus Literary Editing"; "20102. Describe procedure in the event of chopped-down/slurred speech"; "30303. Discuss best practices in the event of tics and mannerisms vs. substantive words"; "30304. Describe procedure when handling indiscernible speech"; "30306. Describe importance of transcribing verbatim". Grammar and punctuation are 30% of the exam. — [AAERT CET Blueprint (PDF)](https://aaert.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/07/CET-Examination-Blueprint-Updated-062926.pdf)
- [COURT RULE, verified] Washington GR 35 accepts AAERT certification as a qualification to produce "an official verbatim report of proceedings of an electronically recorded court proceeding". — [Wash. GR 35](https://www.courts.wa.gov/court_rules/pdf/GR/GA_GR_35_00_00.pdf)
- [JUDICIARY POLICY, verified] The federal certificate for ESR transcripts is signed by a "court-approved transcriber" certifying "a correct transcript from the official electronic sound recording". So in federal court the legal record is the recording, and the transcriber's text is a certified rendering of it. — [Guide Vol. 6 § 520.63.30(c), D. Mont. copy](https://www.mtd.uscourts.gov/sites/mtd/files/Transcript%20Format%202020.pdf)
- [SEARCH SUMMARY ONLY - NOT VERIFIED] AAERT sells a "Best Practices Guide" with transcript standards. Its text is not public, and its definition of verbatim could not be checked. — [AAERT certification program](https://aaert.org/become-certified/certification-program/)

### Inferences
- Under both 28 U.S.C. § 753(b) ("electronic sound recording, or any other method") and the NY vendor scheme, the *recording* is the original record when ESR or FTR is used, and the human transcript is derivative. That is the natural legal slot an ASR engine would fill, as a transcriber of the official recording, subject to the same certification and the same "prima facie" rebuttability.
- Strongest citations: § 753(b) and Guide § 520.63.30(c) for federal ESR; Wash. GR 35 as an example of a state rule recognizing AAERT; the AAERT CET blueprint as professional-standard evidence (weaker, since it is a certification document, not a rule).

### Gaps
- No AAERT published definition of "verbatim", "clean verbatim" or rules on filler words was found in a freely available primary source.
- No published accuracy figures for digital court recording (FTR) systems from a standards body were found in this pass. (See `asr-capstone/vendor-claims.md` for vendor claims.)
