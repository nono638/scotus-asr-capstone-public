# Every transcript certifies one listener's judgement

A "true and accurate" transcript is not a copy of speech. It is a certified opinion about speech, and the law treats it as one: federal law makes the reporter's certified transcript only **"prima facie a correct statement"** ([28 U.S.C. § 753(b)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/28/753)). Where the record is disputed, the trial judge settles it, not the reporter ([FRAP 10(e)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/rules/frap/rule_10); [CPLR 5525(c)](https://newyork.public.law/laws/n.y._civil_practice_law_and_rules_section_5525)), and the profession's own ethics body defines the record as **"what the reporter heard and reported"** ([NCRA Advisory Opinion 47](https://ncra.org/home/the-profession/NCRA-Code-of-Professional-Ethics/COPE-Advisory-Opinions/group-page-(advisory-opinions)/Advisory-Opinion-47)). Every standard examined (court format rules, interpreter codes, parliamentary Hansards, vendor style guides, ASR benchmarks) says the words must be recorded "as spoken". Each one then builds in a judgement about meaning. The federal rule drops false starts and "uhms" unless leaving them out "could change a statement's meaning". Hansard drops "repetitions and redundancies" but "leaves out nothing that adds to the meaning". Rev's clean style edits "lightly" without changing "meaning". So the question is not whether a transcriber interprets but *where the rule lets them*. Linguistics and perception research agree that bias-free transcription is impossible: listeners restore missing sounds from context, hear accents they expect, and spell dialect in ways that stigmatise. The debate is ancient. Thucydides (c. 400 BC) chose "the general sense" over word-for-word precision, Roman stenographers kept a verbatim copy of Cicero's *Pro Milone* beside his polished one, and an Old Bailey shorthand writer in 1754 said he took down "the proper evidence" and dropped imperfect answers. In ASR research the "correct" transcript is a vendor's style guide. WER normalisation, which deletes fillers and expands "gonna" before scoring, is editing applied after the fact, and OpenAI admits it tuned its normaliser to Whisper's own style. That supports the capstone thesis directly: on Supreme Court, CORAAL, EdAcc and Earnings-22 audio, some of the gap between engines and reporters measures a difference in transcription philosophy, not in hearing.

**How to read the tags.** After key sources the report gives *(type; status)*. Types: statute, court rule, judiciary policy, case, professional code, practitioner guidance, official guidance, peer-reviewed, preprint, primary ancient/early-modern text, book, blog, encyclopedia. Status: **verified** means the research team read the quoted wording in the source itself (or a court- or government-hosted reproduction); **second-hand** means it was seen only as quoted or summarised by another source, or in a search excerpt; **unverified** means it rests on memory or was not confirmed. The last two sections list the strongest sources and the quotes still needing a library check.

## "True and accurate" means presumed correct until a judge says otherwise

The phrase "true and accurate" itself comes from professional custom, not statute. NCRA's ethics committee says most jurisdictions require the certificate to state that **"the transcript is a true and accurate record of the proceeding"**, and that "No act could undermine the profession more than false certification of a record" ([NCRA AO 19](https://ncra.org/home/the-profession/NCRA-Code-of-Professional-Ethics/COPE-Advisory-Opinions/group-page-(advisory-opinions)/Advisory-Opinion-19); professional code, verified). A reporter may not certify a transcript she did not produce or verify ([AO 34](https://ncra.org/home/the-profession/NCRA-Code-of-Professional-Ethics/COPE-Advisory-Opinions/group-page-(advisory-opinions)/Advisory-Opinion-34); verified). The statutes use plainer words. Federal law requires proceedings to be **"recorded verbatim by shorthand, mechanical means, electronic sound recording, or any other method"**, and the certified transcript "shall be deemed prima facie a correct statement of the testimony taken and proceedings had" ([28 U.S.C. § 753(b)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/28/753); statute, verified). California says the same in different words: a certified transcript "is prima facie evidence of that testimony and proceedings" ([Cal. CCP § 273](https://california.public.law/codes/code_of_civil_procedure_section_273); statute, verified). New York never says "verbatim" in the sections checked. Its stenographer "must take full stenographic notes of the testimony and of all other proceedings" ([Jud. Law § 295](https://newyork.public.law/laws/n.y._judiciary_law_section_295)), the notes themselves "are part of the proceedings in the cause" ([§ 292](https://newyork.public.law/laws/n.y._judiciary_law_section_292)), and the reporter furnishes "a certified transcript" ([§ 300](https://newyork.public.law/laws/n.y._judiciary_law_section_300)) (statutes, verified). No New York statute or rule using the exact words "true and accurate" was found, so that phrasing in New York certificates should be called customary practice (unverified as a legal requirement).

"True according to whom" therefore has a layered answer, and the layers are written into the rules. **First, the reporter's perception**: NCRA AO 47 says the official transcript "is the written record of what the reporter heard and reported", and conversation picked up by a video microphone but not heard by the reporter stays out unless all parties agree and it is labelled as coming from the video ([AO 47](https://ncra.org/home/the-profession/NCRA-Code-of-Professional-Ethics/COPE-Advisory-Opinions/group-page-(advisory-opinions)/Advisory-Opinion-47); verified). **Second, the parties and the judge**: under FRAP 10(e) "any difference ... about whether the record truly discloses what occurred" is "settled by that court and the record conformed accordingly" ([FRAP 10](https://www.law.cornell.edu/rules/frap/rule_10); court rule, verified). In New York the appellant proposes amendments, and if the parties disagree the transcript "shall be submitted for settlement to the judge or referee" ([CPLR 5525(c)](https://newyork.public.law/laws/n.y._civil_practice_law_and_rules_section_5525); court rule, verified). Washington writes the judge's power into the certificate itself: "a true and correct record of the proceedings to the best of my ability, including any changes made by the trial judge reviewing the transcript" ([Wash. GR 35](https://www.courts.wa.gov/court_rules/pdf/GR/GA_GR_35_00_00.pdf); court rule, verified). NCRA pulls the other way. A reporter "should not make a change even if requested by the court", **"even those of a grammatical nature"**, unless all counsel agree ([AO 4](https://ncra.org/home/the-profession/NCRA-Code-of-Professional-Ethics/COPE-Advisory-Opinions/group-page-(advisory-opinions)/Advisory-Opinion-4); verified). **Third, the recording**, where one is the official record: the federal certificate for electronic recording says "a correct transcript from the official electronic sound recording" ([Guide to Judiciary Policy Vol. 6 § 520.63.30, D. Mont. copy](https://www.mtd.uscourts.gov/sites/mtd/files/Transcript%20Format%202020.pdf); judiciary policy, verified). That makes the audio the original and the text a certified rendering of it. Note also the wording gap: a transcriber working from a recording or someone else's notes certifies "to the best of my ability", but the stenographer's certificate makes no such admission.

The courts set the legal floor lower than the professional ideal. In *United States v. Selva* the Fifth Circuit called § 753(b) "mandatory", but reversal turns on "the absence of a substantial and significant portion of the record" (there, the prosecutor's closing) ([559 F.2d 1303 (5th Cir. 1977)](https://m.openjurist.org/559/f2d/1303); case, verified). The researchers found **no reported appeal that turned on a reporter correcting grammar or recording what a witness meant**. Such disputes go to record settlement, not reversal. The Supreme Court is no exception. Its transcripts are made under contract by Heritage Reporting Corporation, and "Same-day transcripts are considered official but subject to final review" ([supremecourt.gov](https://www.supremecourt.gov/oral_arguments/argument_transcript/2022); official website, verified). Until October Term 2004, questions from the bench were labelled only "Question". The Court changed this "in the interest of the accuracy and completeness of the transcripts" ([SCOTUS press release, 28 Sept 2004](https://www.supremecourt.gov/publicinfo/press/pressreleases/pr_09-28-04); official, verified). No published SCOTUS policy on fillers, false starts or corrections was found. For the capstone, this means the SCOTUS reference is a professional reporter's provisional product, and the paper should describe it as a reference, not as ground truth.

One more point bears on the NCRA material. Every advisory opinion says members follow it only "to the extent that such practices are consistent with their own applicable state and local laws" ([AO 4](https://ncra.org/home/the-profession/NCRA-Code-of-Professional-Ethics/COPE-Advisory-Opinions/group-page-(advisory-opinions)/Advisory-Opinion-4); verified). ## Every rule says "as spoken" and then asks for a judgement about meaning

The federal format rule is the clearest statement, and it is binding: "No court, judge, supervisor, reporter, or transcriber may authorize a deviation" ([Guide Vol. 6 § 520.10, D. Mont. copy](https://www.mtd.uscourts.gov/sites/mtd/files/Transcript%20Format%202020.pdf); judiciary policy, verified). Section 520.40.10(b), "Editing of Speech", has two halves. First, **"All grammatical errors, changes of thought, contractions, misstatements, and poorly constructed sentences must be transcribed as spoken."** Second, "In the interest of readability, false starts, stutters, uhms and ahs, and other verbal tics are not normally included in transcripts; but such verbalizations must be transcribed **whenever their exclusion could change a statement's meaning**" ([same](https://www.mtd.uscourts.gov/sites/mtd/files/Transcript%20Format%202020.pdf)). An older copy of the same section says "should" where the current one says "must" ([N.D. Ind. copy](https://www.innd.uscourts.gov/sites/innd/files/Solicitation%20-%20J7%20-%20Guide%20Vol%206%20Section%20520%20-%20Transcripts.pdf); verified). So the federal "verbatim" standard is word-first for grammar but meaning-gated for disfluency, and the gate is the reporter's judgement. In ASR terms, the federal default is close to **"clean verbatim"**: fillers out, wrong words kept. An engine that drops "uh" is compliant; one that fixes "he don't" to "he doesn't" is not. Texas and New Jersey copy the "as spoken" sentence but leave out the false-start exception, and Texas adds that "Uh-huh" and "Huh-uh" "should be transcribed accordingly" ([Texas UFM](https://www.txcourts.gov/jbcc/court-reporters-certification/statutes-rules-and-resources-for-court-reporters-firms/uniform-format-manual/); [NJ format](https://www.njcourts.gov/sites/default/files/courts/transcript.pdf); court rules, verified). Even within the United States, then, "verbatim" means different things in different courts.

Court interpreters work under the opposite emphasis, and the contrast sharpens the thesis. The NCSC Model Code, Canon 1, requires rendering "without altering, omitting, or adding anything to what is stated", but its commentary says **"Verbatim, 'word for word,' or literal oral interpretations are not appropriate when they distort the meaning of the source language"**, while "every spoken statement, even if it appears non-responsive, obscene, rambling, or incoherent should be interpreted. This includes apparent misstatements" ([NCSC Model Code, Ch. 9, Delaware Courts copy](https://www.courts.delaware.gov/forms/download.aspx?id=134438); professional code, verified). Washington's adaptation adds "false starts" and requires "the closest natural equivalent" ([Wash. GR 11.2](https://www.courts.wa.gov/court_rules/pdf/GR/GA_GR_11_02_00.pdf); court rule, verified). A common paraphrase of the interpreter rule ("including hesitations, false starts, repetitions") does **not** appear in the NCSC text. "False starts" is Washington's word and "hesitations" was found in neither, so quote with care. Both systems tell the professional to keep the speaker's errors and to judge meaning. They just put the default on opposite sides.

Commercial style guides land in the same place. Rev's guide, the one behind the Earnings-21/22 references, says for both of its styles: **"Do not write what you think the speaker meant to say"** and "Never correct or edit a speaker's grammar". Its default non-verbatim style then makes exceptions: it must "lightly edit for readability" without changing "the structure or meaning", always turn "gonna", "'cause" and "'em" into "going to", "because" and "them", and correct "egregious phonetic and pronunciation" slips ("refrigurator" becomes "refrigerator") ([Rev Transcription Style Guide v4.0.x](https://cf-public.rev.com/styleguide/transcription/Rev+Transcription+Style+Guide+v4.0.1.pdf); practitioner guidance, verified). That is a dialect-levelling edit inside a style that says it never corrects grammar. The empirical evidence that human professionals edit in practice comes from Hennink & Weber. Comparing court reporters with transcriptionists on 16 focus-group transcripts, they found court reporters "were less able to produce a verbatim transcript" and grammatically corrected speech: "diabetes ain't so obvious" became "diabetes is not so obvious" ([Hennink & Weber 2013, *Qualitative Health Research*](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4465445/); peer-reviewed, verified). The setting was not a courtroom, so it shows habit, not courtroom practice. The foundational forensic-linguistics critique of the "verbatim" court record is Anne Graffam Walker's: the record rests on a "necessary presumption" that exact words can be written down, modified by reporters' customs, and appellate judges read only the edited product ([Walker 1990, in *Language in the Judicial Process*](https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1007/978-1-4899-3719-3_7); book chapter, abstract only, second-hand). Haworth et al. summarise the whole line, Walker, Eades, Tiersma, Shuy and Fraser: **"All such studies have found serious problems with the official transcripts produced"** ([Haworth et al. 2023, *Frontiers in Communication*](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/communication/articles/10.3389/fcomm.2023.1178516/full); peer-reviewed, verified).

So should a transcriber record what was spoken or what was meant? Every legal and professional text answers "spoken" in its headline and "meant" in its exceptions. The honest description is that the rules fix *which* judgements a transcriber may make (whether a filler matters, whether a slip is "obvious", whether a pronunciation is "egregious") and leave the judging itself to the listener. That is exactly the capstone's distinction between transcribers who use context to resolve ambiguity and those who stay literal.

## Legislatures wrote editing into the definition of "verbatim"

Parliaments have been explicit about what courts leave implicit. The Westminster definition, cited to Erskine May (25th ed., para 7.19), calls the Official Report a "full report", one **"which, though not strictly verbatim, is substantially the verbatim report, with repetitions and redundancies omitted and with obvious mistakes corrected, but which on the other hand leaves out nothing that adds to the meaning of the speech or illustrates the argument"** ([BC Hansard Services guide, 2024](https://members.leg.bc.ca/wp-content/uploads/2024/10/Hansard-Publications-and-Guidelines-for-Corrections-by-Members-Revisions-2024.pdf); official guidance quoting Erskine May, verified). The Commonwealth Hansard Editors Association attributes it to the 1907 Select Committee ([CHEA](https://www.commonwealth-hansard.org/about-hansard.html); professional association, verified). Wikipedia instead says 1893 and adds "(including grammatical mistakes)" ([Wikipedia, "Hansard"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hansard); encyclopedia). The likeliest reading is that an 1893 committee recommended a "full" report and the 1907 committee wrote the definition, but that is an inference not yet checked against the committee report. The minister who put it into practice was blunter: a full report "means a verbatim report, trimmed of all those excrescences and redundancies", and "puts into something like literary shape the efforts with which we endeavour to express our thoughts". A colleague objected that "literary shape" was "rather a large order" ([HC Deb 14 May 1908 vol 188 cc1357–67](https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/commons/1908/may/14/class-ii); primary parliamentary record, verified).

Practice goes further than the definition. British Columbia's guide licenses removal of "Repetitions, false starts, and self-corrections" and correction of "obvious errors (i.e. those that an average listener would recognize ...)", provided the error "did not give rise to discussion in the House" ([BC guide](https://members.leg.bc.ca/wp-content/uploads/2024/10/Hansard-Publications-and-Guidelines-for-Corrections-by-Members-Revisions-2024.pdf); verified). The "average listener" is an explicit listener-judgement test with no counterpart in court rhetoric. Australia sets the editing level by context: chamber speeches get a "readable" rendition, committee witnesses "should, by and large, be given their exact words", and **"Politically sensitive subjects require a more strictly verbatim approach"** ([Parliament of Australia, Hansard](https://www.aph.gov.au/Parliamentary_Business/Hansard/Search); official guidance, verified). That last sentence concedes that ordinary editing can change meaning in ways that matter. The peer-reviewed comparisons confirm it. Mollin compared Hansard with her own transcript of a recorded Commons session and found it omits hesitations and contextual talk and that editors **"alter speakers' lexical and grammatical choices towards more conservative and formal variants"** ([Mollin 2007, *Corpora*](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.3366/cor.2007.2.2.187); peer-reviewed, abstract verified). Slembrouck (1992) is the foundational study but was read only through later summaries ([Benjamins bibliography](https://benjamins.com/online/ebop/publications/38287); second-hand). Kawahara gives numbers: **about 13% of words edited in the Japanese Diet record and over 20% in the European Parliament's English**, almost 90% of Diet edits being deletions and half of those filler removal. Diet editing fell 40% between 2007 and 2016 after reporters began editing an ASR draft rather than typing from shorthand "with editing in mind" ([Kawahara 2024, ParlaCLARIN IV](https://aclanthology.org/2024.parlaclarin-1.10.pdf); peer-reviewed workshop, verified). Technology changed the editorial norm, not just the speed.

The US Congressional Record departs from verbatim in the other direction: little editing of what was said, but insertion of what was never said. The statute requires "substantially a verbatim report" ([44 U.S.C. 901, GPO](https://www.govinfo.gov/media/rules.txt); statute, verified). The House allows only "technical, grammatical and typographical corrections", and inserted remarks appear in distinctive type, a bullet in the Senate ([House Manual §§ 686–687](https://www.gpo.gov/fdsys/pkg/HMAN-107/html/HMAN-107-pg378.htm); official rule, verified). Before 1985, though, a member who spoke *any* part of a speech could revise the whole "to appear as if actually delivered" ([same](https://www.gpo.gov/fdsys/pkg/HMAN-107/html/HMAN-107-pg378.htm)). In *Gregg v. Barrett* the D.C. Circuit held "There simply is no first amendment right to receive a verbatim transcript of the proceedings of Congress", and quoted an advisory committee contrasting the Record with **"a completely verbatim transcript such as is produced in a courtroom"** ([771 F.2d 539 (D.C. Cir. 1985)](https://m.openjurist.org/771/f2d/539); case, verified). That is a citable judicial statement that court and legislative reporting follow different norms. The UN goes further. Its Security Council verbatim record contains "the original text of speeches delivered in English and **interpretations** of speeches delivered in the other languages" ([UN S/PV.3721](https://digitallibrary.un.org/record/229173/files/S_PV.3721-EN.pdf); official, verified). UN Geneva checks corrections "against the audio recordings of the meeting" ([UN Geneva FAQ](https://www.ungeneva.org/en/faqs/how-can-i-ensure-my-contribution-meeting-properly-reflected-record); official guidance, verified). There the recording, not the transcript, is the authority.

## The debate is 2,400 years old: precision of words versus the general sense

Ancient writers set out both positions. Thucydides found it "difficult to carry them word for word in one's memory", so he made speakers say what the occasion demanded, **"adhering as closely as possible to the general sense of what they really said"** ([Thucydides 1.22.1, Crawley trans., Project Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/7142/pg7142.txt); primary ancient text, verified). The Greek gives the paper its two terms: *tēn akribeian autēn tōn lechthentōn*, "the exact precision of what was said", which he could not reach, against *tēs xumpasēs gnōmēs*, "the overall sense", which he adopted ([Greek text, PerseusDL](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/PerseusDL/canonical-greekLit/master/data/tlg0003/tlg001/tlg0003.tlg001.perseus-grc2.xml); verified, the gloss is the research team's own). Thucydides held reports of *events* to a stricter test than speeches, and he blamed eyewitness disagreement on "imperfect memory" and "undue partiality for one side or the other" ([Crawley](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/7142/pg7142.txt)). That is an ancient statement that witnesses are biased listeners. The popular claim that Xenophon invented shorthand rests on one ambiguous verb in a 3rd-century AD biography, which Hicks translates simply as "take notes of" ([Diogenes Laertius 2.48, Wikisource](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Lives_of_the_Eminent_Philosophers/Book_II); primary, verified). Treat it as legend.

Rome supplies the "two texts" problem. Plutarch reports, hedging twice with "we are told", that Cicero placed clerks trained in shorthand signs "in various parts of the senate-house" to take down Cato's speech in 63 BC ([Plutarch, *Cato Minor* 23.3, Loeb/LacusCurtius](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Plutarch/Lives/Cato_Minor*.html); primary, verified). It was an early form of redundant capture. Asconius, writing about a century after the trial, says the shorthand copy of Cicero's faltering *Pro Milone* survived, **"Manet autem illa quoque excepta eius oratio"**, alongside the polished version "which we read" ([Asconius, Latin Library](https://www.thelatinlibrary.com/asconius.html); primary, verified). Cassius Dio adds Milo's joke from exile: had that speech actually been delivered, he would not be eating such fine mullets in Massilia ([Dio 40.54, LacusCurtius](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Cassius_Dio/40*.html); primary, verified, but written about 250 years later). Seneca dismissed shorthand, signs "matching speed of tongue by speed of hand", as devised "by the lowest grade of slaves" ([Seneca *Ep.* 90.25, Gummere trans.](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Moral_letters_to_Lucilius/Letter_90); primary, verified). That is the ancient form of the hierarchy that ranks mechanical capture below understanding, the same hierarchy that now ranks ASR below the reporter. The strongest ancient precedent for a *certified* record is the Conference of Carthage in AD 411. Both sides' stenographers "compared their notes and agreed on a final text, which in turn had to be approved by the recorded speakers", and the surviving text keeps a bishop's repeated grammatical self-corrections, which suggests the record leaned verbatim ([Riedlberger 2020, review of Weidmann's CSEL 104 edition, *Plekos*](https://www.plekos.uni-muenchen.de/2020/r-weidmann.pdf); peer-reviewed review, verified). Augustine's sermons survive because hearers' stenographers took them down live, but only some were revised by him, and scholars still debate their accuracy ([van Oort, *HTS Teologiese Studies*](https://www.scielo.org.za/pdf/hts/v65n1/50.pdf); peer-reviewed, second-hand). The Fournier inquisition register (1318–25) is the medieval case of a transcript that is really a translation. Occitan testimony became quick notes, then Latin minutes read back to the accused, and later a French and an English translation ([Wikipedia, Fournier Register](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fournier_Register); encyclopedia, use only as a pointer).

The early modern record is the most direct precedent for court reporting. Thomas Heywood complained that pirates took down his play "by stenography" and printed it "scarce one word true" ([*Early Theatre* article](https://prod-mulpress.lib.mcmaster.ca/earlytheatre/article/view/1203/2329); peer-reviewed, quote second-hand). Two shorthand-derived transcripts of the 1648 Whitehall Debates differ, and the newly found one "clarifies obscure passages" ([Polizzotto, *Historical Journal*](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/historical-journal/article/abs/what-really-happened-at-the-whitehall-debates-a-new-source/93B7E4AC10CDD705D7935374EEA3AA70); peer-reviewed, abstract). The Old Bailey Proceedings began presenting trials "as verbatim transcripts" in the 1720s, yet Richard Savage's eight-hour trial became 2,447 words ([Shoemaker 2008, *Journal of British Studies*](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/4697/1/shoemakerr2.pdf); peer-reviewed, verified). The official shorthand writer Thomas Gurney, testifying in 1754, stated the clean-verbatim philosophy almost word for word: **"if a question brings out an imperfect answer, and is obliged to be asked over again, and the answer comes more strong, I take that down as the proper evidence, and neglect the other. . . . It is not to be expected I should write every unintelligible word"** ([Shoemaker 2008, quoting *The Trial of Elizabeth Canning*](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/4697/1/shoemakerr2.pdf); peer-reviewed quoting a primary text, verified as quoted). In 1775 John Wilkes reported that judges complained the shorthand writer published "without any control or revision" and that parts were "contrary to his own notes". By 1778 the City required "a true, fair, and perfect narrative of the whole evidence", which is strikingly close to today's "true and accurate" ([Shoemaker 2008](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/4697/1/shoemakerr2.pdf)). Selectivity also took sides. In Catherine Hayes's case, prosecution testimony appeared verbatim while her defence was summarised "with some crucial testimony left out" ([same](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/4697/1/shoemakerr2.pdf)). Samuel Johnson shows the far end of "sense over words". Sometimes given only "the names of the several speakers, and the part which they had taken", he wrote debates for the *Gentleman's Magazine*, and later regretted "having been the authour of fictions which had passed for realities" ([Boswell, *Life of Johnson*, Osgood abridged ed.](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/1564/pg1564.txt); primary, the regret is in an editorial note, so second-hand). Fluent invented speech passed off as a record is the historical ancestor of ASR hallucination.

## No neutral transcript exists, for humans or machines

The linguistics literature settled the bias question decades ago. Ochs called transcription "a selective process reflecting theoretical goals and definitions" (1979: 44) ([quoted in *FQS*](https://qualitative-research.net/index.php/fqs/article/download/1357/2943/5277); book chapter, second-hand). Bucholtz split the transcriber's choices into *interpretation* (what to write) and *representation* (how), and argued that both "naturalized" transcription (made to look like writing) and "denaturalized" transcription (keeping oral features) **can serve as politicised tools**, so neither is the neutral option ([Bucholtz 2000, *Journal of Pragmatics*](https://escholarship.org/content/qt6581373n/qt6581373n.pdf); peer-reviewed, scanned PDF so summary-level only). Oliver, Serovich & Mason give the clearest one-liner, **"a transcriber hears the interview through his/her own cultural-linguistic filters"**, plus a perfect example of context resolving ambiguity: the team "assumed" a participant's "annual" meant "anal". The transcriber also wanted to fix "laying" to "lying" for the interviewer but not the participant, so correction followed status ([Oliver et al. 2005, *Social Forces*](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1400594); peer-reviewed, verified). A terminology warning for the paper: Bucholtz's "naturalized" (made to look like writing) is close to Oliver's "denaturalism", not to Oliver's "naturalism". Pick one pair and define it. In forensic work, Haworth et al. state flatly that a "perfect" transcript "is an impossibility" and that "it is impossible for any transcriber not to bring in their own perspectives and unconscious biases". They also report that people who *read* an interview transcript judged the speaker less credible than people who *heard* the audio ([Haworth et al. 2023](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/communication/articles/10.3389/fcomm.2023.1178516/full); peer-reviewed, verified). Format alone changes outcomes.

Perception research explains why. In Warren's phonemic-restoration experiment, a cough replaced the first /s/ of "legislatures". Listeners heard the whole word and could not say where the cough fell, while a silent gap produced no illusion ([Warren 1970, *Science*; MIT course copy](https://web.mit.edu/9.35/www/archives/2020/auditory/papers/Warren_PhonemicRestoration.pdf); peer-reviewed, verified). Human hearing fills gaps with the probable word, which is what an ASR language model does. Expectations about the speaker also change what is heard: students shown an Asian face with an identical native-English lecture rated it more accented and understood less, a process Kang & Rubin name "reverse linguistic stereotyping" ([NAU summary](https://news.nau.edu/?p=52088); university press, second-hand). Fraser's forensic work shows that a supplied transcript primes listeners to hear its words in indistinct audio ([cited in Haworth et al. 2023](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/communication/articles/10.3389/fcomm.2023.1178516/full); second-hand).

Court reporters are not exempt, and the evidence is strongest for African American English. Jones, Kalbfeld, Hancock & Clark tested Philadelphia court reporters, certified at 95% accuracy, on everyday AAE and found they often fell below that standard and often did not understand what was said ([Jones et al. 2019, *Language*](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/language/article/abs/testifying-while-black-an-experimental-study-of-court-reporter-accuracy-in-transcription-of-african-american-english/BF02EDFAE96A5E3EB26DDF5260CC187C); peer-reviewed, abstract verified). The first author's own write-up gives the numbers: 27 reporters, **82.9% mean word accuracy and 59.5% sentence accuracy**, none reaching 95%, and errors that changed "who, what, when, or where" in 31% of sentences. "He don't be in that neighborhood" became "We going to be in this neighborhood". Race, experience and education of the reporter did not predict accuracy ([languagejones.com](https://www.languagejones.com/blog-1/tag/justice); blog by the lead author, so practitioner-level until checked against the article). That last finding supports framing bias as exposure and expectation, not individual prejudice, which fits the "one listener's judgement" thesis. Rickford & King show the cost in a real trial: Rachel Jeantel's testimony in the Zimmerman case was dismissed as unintelligible, though it was systematic AAVE ([Rickford & King 2016, *Language*](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/language/article/abs/language-and-linguistics-on-trial-hearing-rachel-jeantel-and-other-vernacular-speakers-in-the-courtroom-and-beyond/7DCD0B43230E3D17A2C4B43936341A48); peer-reviewed, abstract verified). Spelling offers no safe choice. Dialect respellings make readers assume low status "uncritically and spontaneously" ([Jaffe & Walton 2000, *Journal of Sociolinguistics*](https://benjamins.com/online/ebop/publications/15894); peer-reviewed, abstract-level), and Preston opposed such respellings ([Preston's ADS-L post](https://americandialect.org/americandialectarchives/aug94067.html); listserv). Yet standard spelling invites "correcting" grammar such as habitual *be*. Unbiased transcription is therefore not available. What a transcriber can offer is **documented bias**: stated conventions, audio kept linked to text, and disclosure of choices. That is the reflexive practice Bucholtz and Davidson call for ([Davidson 2009, *IJQM*](https://ejournals.library.ualberta.ca/index.php/IJQM/article/view/4205); peer-reviewed, abstract-level).

## ASR's "ground truth" is a style guide, and normalisation is editing after the fact

### The industry sells three or four kinds of "correct"

No vendor standard exists. Verbit itself says there is "no universally-accepted set of clean verbatim transcription rules". It also says clean verbatim "wouldn't be appropriate or even allowed" for official legal proceedings ([Verbit blog](https://verbit.ai/general/how-and-when-to-use-clean-verbatim-transcription); blog). GoTranscript sells four tiers, from full verbatim ("every word and sound") through intelligent verbatim to an "edited" tier that "Repairs obvious grammar mistakes" ([GoTranscript blog](https://gotranscript.com/blog/verbatim-transcription); blog). Rev's verbatim style keeps "filler words, stutters, interjections (active listening) and repetitions", and its default non-verbatim style edits them out, turning "And so, um, I guess... I think we should go to the, the m- m- movies tonight 'cause of the discount (laughs)" into "I think we should go to the movies tonight because of the discount" ([Rev Style Guide](https://cf-public.rev.com/styleguide/transcription/Rev+Transcription+Style+Guide+v4.0.1.pdf); practitioner guidance, verified). Rev now trains a single model on 120,000 hours of verbatim and 80,000 hours of non-verbatim labels with a "verbatimicity parameter" between zero and one. **Literalness has become a dial** ([Bhandari et al. 2024, arXiv:2410.03930](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.03930); preprint, verified).

### Each capstone corpus encodes its own convention

The capstone's four references follow four philosophies. **Earnings-22**'s builders "chose to produce verbatim transcriptions to best model real human speech", using Rev's human service and style guide ([Del Rio et al. 2022, arXiv:2203.15591](https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.15591); preprint, verified). Earnings-21 explained the choice: "verbatim transcripts are more useful for ASR evaluation" ([Del Rio et al. 2021, Interspeech](https://arxiv.org/abs/2104.11348); peer-reviewed, verified). **CORAAL** states that a transcript "is not to make an exact, textually accurate representation of the speech". It keeps morphosyntactic variants ("was for were", "friends with they kids") but not phonological ones (in for ing, r-lessness). It writes "talmbout" as "talking about" and, where a feature is ambiguous, errs toward standard spelling ([CORAAL User Guide 2018.10.06](https://talkbank.org/ca/access/0docs/CORAAL.pdf); corpus documentation, verified). **EdAcc** was transcribed by professionals "including ... hesitations". The authors then removed punctuation, normalised spelling, and ignored laughs and numbers in scoring, while the public release "does not contain any normalization" ([Sanabria et al. 2023, arXiv:2303.18110](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.18110); preprint/ICASSP, verified). Anyone rescoring EdAcc therefore makes new editorial choices. **SCOTUS** transcripts are a contract reporter's legal-record genre, "subject to final review", with no published filler policy.

### Human "error rates" depend on method and style

The speech community has measured human disagreement directly. LDC calls transcriber-to-transcriber differences a "Word Disagreement Rate" **"Since not all of the transcription 'errors' are truly mistakes"**. Careful transcription of English telephone speech disagreed 4.1–4.5% and quick transcription 9.63%, and 95% of careful-transcription discrepancies were "judgment calls" over contractions, rapid speech or disfluencies. For quick transcription, "omitting a disfluency is considered insignificant" ([Glenn et al. 2010, LREC](http://www.lrec-conf.org/proceedings/lrec2010/pdf/849_Paper.pdf); peer-reviewed, verified). Microsoft's "human parity" claim measured 5.9% (Switchboard) and 11.3% (CallHome) with an ordinary two-pass vendor pipeline ([Xiong et al. 2016, arXiv:1610.05256](https://arxiv.org/abs/1610.05256); preprint, verified). IBM, with three transcribers plus a senior checker, got 5.1% and 6.8% and concluded that careful human WER "is simply lower than what was previously reported" ([Saon et al. 2017, arXiv:1703.02136](https://arxiv.org/abs/1703.02136); preprint/Interspeech, verified). Use the abstract figures for Xiong et al., since the paper's conclusion repeats the machine numbers as human ones. The closest study to the capstone is Rev's own. McNamara et al. made verbatim and non-verbatim references for a 10-hour Earnings-22 subset, and the OpenAI (Whisper-derived) API scored **13.11% WER against verbatim but 6.41% against non-verbatim**, while Parakeet-TDT-1.1B moved only from 14.94% to 12.16% ([McNamara et al. 2024, arXiv:2412.07937](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.07937); preprint, verified; Rev authors, so a possible home-team bias). They conclude that existing WER reports "are likely significantly over-estimating the number of contentful errors". They do not report the WER between the two references themselves. The capstone's own measured 13.4% gap between two Earnings-22 references (an unpublished project result) is larger than every published human-vs-human figure above, which fits a difference in style rules on top of ordinary mishearing. The reversal in the Reverb paper should be flagged: on Rev16, Whisper large-v3 scores slightly *better* against verbatim (10.67 vs 11.37) ([Bhandari et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.03930)). Undisclosed scoring normalisation is the likely cause, and it is unresolved.

### Normalisation edits the reference toward one engine's style

Yes, ASR normalisation is a form of editing, and its authors say so in effect. OpenAI built Whisper's normaliser "through iterative manual inspection to identify common patterns where naive WER penalized Whisper models for an innocuous difference", and warned it "comes at a risk of overfitting to the transcription style of Whisper models" ([Radford et al. 2023, ICML](https://arxiv.org/abs/2212.04356); peer-reviewed, verified). The code deletes `hmm|mm|mhm|mmm|uh|um` and rewrites `gonna → going to`, `wanna → want to`, `gotta → got to` ([openai/whisper english.py](https://github.com/openai/whisper/blob/main/whisper/normalizers/english.py); code, verified). Those are the same edits Rev's clean style makes and close to Hansard's "repetitions and redundancies omitted". The Open ASR Leaderboard applies a Whisper-like pipeline to every model and itself suggests separating tasks by "whether ... verbatim transcription is required" ([Srivastav et al. 2025, arXiv:2510.06961](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.06961); preprint, verified). ESB took the opposite view and kept punctuation and casing for an "orthographic WER", arguing that dataset-specific normalisation assumes prior knowledge of the text distribution ([Gandhi et al. 2022, arXiv:2210.13352](https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.13352); preprint, verified). Older tools also wrote leniency into scoring. NIST's sclite marks hesitations as optionally deletable ([SCTK docs](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/usnistgov/SCTK/master/doc/sclite.htm); documentation, second-hand detail), and Rev's fstalign treats "going to" and "gonna" as penalty-free synonyms ([Del Rio et al. 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2104.11348); verified).

Normalisation also shapes the bias literature. Koenecke et al. found average WER of 0.35 for Black speakers and 0.19 for white speakers across five commercial systems. They attribute the gap mainly to acoustic models, but before scoring they changed "aks" to "ask", normalised "gonna" and "tryna", and removed fillers ([Koenecke et al. 2020, *PNAS*; PMC full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7149386/); peer-reviewed, verified). Martin & Tang show the grammatical route: habitual *be* is more error-prone, plausibly because the language model infers "I be in my office by 7:30" as "I'll be" ([Martin & Tang 2020, Interspeech](https://www.isca-archive.org/interspeech_2020/martin20_interspeech.pdf); peer-reviewed, verified). That is machine context-resolution, the same move as the court reporter who heard "he don't be" as "we going to be". On CORAAL, which keeps grammar but standardises pronunciation, a model that "fixes" "we was" to "we were" is penalised, while one that ignores r-lessness is not. **A CORAAL WER gap therefore partly measures grammatical standardisation, not just mishearing.** No peer-reviewed study yet measures Whisper or Parakeet rewriting AAE grammar as a style effect, so this is open ground for the capstone. Hallucination is a different category. Roughly 1% of Whisper transcriptions contained whole invented phrases, and 38% of those carried explicit harms ([Koenecke et al. 2024, FAccT, "Careless Whisper"](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.08021); peer-reviewed, verified). That is invention, not tidying, and it is Johnson's garret rather than Gurney's "proper evidence". The newest work names the underlying problem. Choi et al. argue that "Ground truth transcripts are not discovered - they are produced by human annotators following conventions", that verbatim, non-verbatim and **legal** conventions judge the same output differently, and that results should be reported as a "WER-Range" across legitimate conventions ([Choi et al. 2026, arXiv:2605.07084](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.07084); preprint, abstract only, with Rev/Earnings-22 co-authors). Semantic metrics such as SemDist offer a complementary check that weights meaning rather than literal tokens ([Kim et al. 2021, arXiv:2104.02138](https://arxiv.org/abs/2104.02138); preprint/Interspeech).

## Conclusion

The sources do more than support the capstone thesis. They suggest a sharper version of it: **every transcription standard is a rule about which listener judgements are allowed and who may overrule them**. Courts let the reporter judge whether a filler matters and let the judge overrule the reporter. Hansard lets the editor judge what "adds to the meaning" and lets the speaker request corrections within limits. Rev lets the transcriber judge what is "egregious". Benchmarks let the normaliser's author judge what is "innocuous", and that author tuned it on one engine. On this view, "verbatim versus clean" is not a contest between truth and editing. It is a contest between two kinds of authorised judgement. A court reporter is unusually well placed to make the argument, because the profession's own documents (AO 47's "what the reporter heard", § 520's "could change a statement's meaning", the certificate's "prima facie") already concede it.

The practical consequences follow. Scoring engines against a single reference measures agreement with one convention, so the capstone should report WER both with and without filler and contraction normalisation, and against more than one reference where one exists. Errors should be classified by kind: tidying (dropped fillers, expanded contractions), standardising (grammar rewritten toward Standard English), mishearing, and invention. Only the last three threaten the "as spoken" rule that courts actually enforce, and invention and standardising are the ones with legal and racial stakes. Kawahara's finding that ASR drafts made the Japanese Diet record *more* literal points to a further question the capstone can raise: if courts adopt machine first drafts, the professional norm of what counts as "true and accurate" will probably shift toward the literal. The 1754 Old Bailey reporter and the 2026 benchmark designer would then have traded places.
